| Score abbreviation "Cl." | Rimsky-Korsakov writes "2 Ob. + 2 Cl." | [Cited S1] |

- Family: woodwinds; a single-reed aerophone, the "single-beating-reed Clarinet group" [Cited S2].
- In the score the clarinets sit immediately under the oboes, which Forsyth calls "a mere accident, due to the historical priority" of the oboes [Cited S2].
- Clarinets in C, B♭ and A were all orchestral in the classical period; by 1914 the C clarinet is obsolete, which "leaves us the choice of two instruments with the same chromatic compass" (B♭ and A) [Cited S2].

### 2. Sound production
- A cylindrical pipe of wood or ebonite about two feet long, in sections: mouthpiece, barrel, left-hand joint, right-hand joint, bell [Cited S2].
- A single cane reed held on the mouthpiece "table" by a metal ligature; the beating reed opens and closes the gap at the rate set by the air column [Cited S2].
- Being a cylindrical reed pipe it acts as a stopped pipe: its fundamental is an octave below an open or conical pipe of the same length, and it "overblows a twelfth", so its fingering differs fundamentally from flute and oboe [Cited S2].
- Seven holes and 13 to 19 keys in 1914 [Cited S2]; the modern soprano has seven tone holes closed directly by the fingers, with 17 keys working the remaining holes [Cited S5].
- The register key moves the player from the chalumeau to the clarion register [Cited S5].
- Boehm ("French") system is standard everywhere except Germany and Austria, where the Oehler ("German") clarinet is used; the two differ in fingering and in sound [Cited S5].
- When played loudly the reed can be shut up to 50 % of the time [Cited S5].

### 3. Range, transposition and clef (sounding and written; comfortable and extreme; register breaks)
- Transposition −2: the B♭ clarinet sounds "a major second lower" and is written two flats fewer or two sharps more than the piece [Cited S2]; Czech "zní o tón níže" [Cited S10].
- Written range E3–C7 (52–96), sounding D3–B♭6 (50–94) [Cited S5]. Berlioz confirms the bottom: the B♭ clarinet's "low E gives only the D" [Cited S4]. Widor: "the B♭ Clarinet stops at D" [Cited S3].
- Top: above the overblown twelfths "seven further semitones are to be got up to high C" (fifth or ninth harmonics); the best players control the tone "right up to the high B♭"; the two top semitones B♮ and C "are just playable" but "so excessively difficult ... and so piercing" that they are better avoided [Cited S2]. Placing Forsyth's high B♭, B and C at written B♭6–C7 (94–96) follows from S5's top of C7 [Estimate: Forsyth's note octave is shown only in an image].
- Widor: compass "42 notes", "A in altissimo" as the upper limit for "the great majority" of players, and C in altissimo "beyond the most persevering efforts of most performers" [Cited S3]. 42 notes ending on written A6 start on written E3 (93 − 52 + 1 = 42), so the practical range is written E3–A6 = sounding 50–91 [Cited S3 for the 42-note count and the "A in altissimo" limit; the MIDI endpoints are arithmetic because Widor's endpoint notes are images].
- Widor adds that "in piano passages" a lower note "should be considered the extreme limit"; that note is an image and could not be read [Cited S3].
- Low extension: some players add a low E♭ key to the B♭ clarinet, sounding D♭ (the A clarinet's lowest note); Forsyth warns not to treat it as "an integral note" [Cited S2]. S5 notes the chalumeau "from the notated E3 (C3 if available)" [Cited S5].
- Clef: always treble, transposing; old scores sometimes wrote chalumeau notes in bass clef an octave low, and Wagner sometimes used bass clef at pitch [Cited S2].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits
| Register | Colour and dynamics | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Chalumeau | "hollow reedy intensity"; not heard in a tutti *mf* or *f*, "overcharged with colour" in thin passages, especially its bottom fifth | [Cited S2] |
| Chalumeau | low register "Ringing, threatening" (Rimsky's table of extreme registers); in the bassoon + clarinet doubling, "the gloomy character of the clarinet prevails in the lower register" | [Cited S1] |
| Low register (Berlioz) | for held notes, "coldly threatening effects ... dark accents of motionless rage" (Weber) | [Cited S4] |
| Throat | "the very worst part of the instrument, the weakest in quality and technically the most difficult"; almost inaudible in *pp*, an ideal background | [Cited S2]; note limits are an image, register attribution [Estimate: as in section 3] |
| Middle | "weak in the middle compass" in mixtures | [Cited S1] |
| Middle (Widor) | "much less characteristic timbre"; "neutral tone-color ... blending with almost every group" | [Cited S3] |
| Clarion | "the best register ... nobly expressive, beautifully clear, pure and even" | [Cited S2] |
| High | "very piercing" | [Cited S3]; "somewhat tearing", for the orchestral *ff* or a brilliant solo, though very high notes "may nevertheless be sustained piano" when prepared [Cited S4] |

- Dynamic range and niente: "no instrument has so complete a command of" crescendo and diminuendo, across the whole compass and the whole family; "the Clarinet *ppp* is the nearest attainable approach to 'nothing at all'" among winds [Cited S2].
- "The pianissimo of the Clarinets (in the low and medium registers) represents the minimum of sound obtainable from wind instruments ... 'tis but a whiff of air" [Cited S3]; the clarinet "alone is able to contrast a piano with a forte" as an echo [Cited S3].
- It "can best breathe forth, swell, diminish, and die away its sound", hence "distance, echo, an echo of echo, and a twilight sound" [Cited S4]; it "can reduce volume of tone to a mere breath" [Cited S1].
- Loud balance: in forte "1 Horn = 2 Clarinets = 2 Oboes = 2 Flutes = 2 Bassoons" [Cited S1].
- Agility: next to the flute the most agile wind; excels in scales (especially chromatic) and arpeggios; C, F, G, B♭ and relatives are easy, difficulties begin with D and E♭ major (written keys) [Cited S3]. Flutes and clarinets are "the most flexible wood-wind instruments" [Cited S1].
- Leaps: "not well adapted to sudden leaps from one octave to another" [Cited S1]; octave-skip passages are "unnatural to cylindrical instruments" [Cited S2]; Mozart exploited skips between registers [Cited S2].
- Repeated notes: cannot beat the flute but reaches "a very respectable speed" [Cited S2].
- Breath limits: no source read gives phrase lengths [Estimate: not documented here; treat long held notes as needing rests for breath like other winds].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)
- Rimsky's scheme: woodwind in pairs = 2 clarinets; in threes = 3 clarinets, with the small and bass clarinet parts played by players II and III; in fours = 3 clarinets plus a separate bass clarinet player (IV) [Cited S1].
- "As a rule two 'Bb-A' instruments are employed", with bass and sometimes E♭ clarinet; Wagner's later works use three plus bass and E♭ [Cited S2].
- "The modern orchestra frequently includes two clarinetists", each usually with a B♭ and an A clarinet; the Romantic section grew to three or more; Strauss's *Elektra* needs up to eight [Cited S5].
- Libraries: BBCSO records a solo clarinet and "a3" (3 players), range D3–E6 [Cited S18]; Orchestral Tools records Clarinet 1 and Clarinet 2 as separate soloists [Cited S16]; Sonatina has "Clarinet Solo" and "Clarinets" ensemble patches [Cited S20].
- Two clarinets in octaves are "not to be recommended" unless strings double them [Cited S1]; for chalumeau harmonies Berlioz prefers separate parts to unison or octaves [Cited S4].
- Woodwind parts are not normally divided like strings; "a2" means both players in unison [Estimate: general score practice; not stated in the sources read].

### 8. Variants and related instruments (when a player changes instrument)
- The player changes to the A clarinet for sharp keys or the low C♯ (see `clarinet_a`) [Cited S2, S5]; the second or third player covers the E♭ or bass clarinet in Rimsky's threes [Cited S1].
- C clarinet: obsolete by 1914, "slightly harder in quality" [Cited S2]; Berlioz finds it "harder than that in B♭" [Cited S4]. B♮ clarinet (Mozart, *Così*, *Idomeneo*) unknown by 1914 [Cited S2].
- Basset horn (F) and alto clarinet (E♭) are rare in the orchestra [Cited S2]; basset clarinet (usually in A) for Mozart's concerto [Cited S5].
- Family span: from the BB♭ contrabass to the A♭ piccolo clarinet [Cited S5].

### 2. Sound production
- Pitched an octave below the standard instrument, "practically an enlarged replica"; a metal crook holds the mouthpiece and a short upturned metal bell projects the sound [Cited S2].
- Long key rods, large reed, wide bore and heavy key work limit agility compared with the B♭ clarinet [Cited S2]; its reed is "a little weaker and more covered" [Cited S4].
- Tone, sources disagree: "a certain windiness and flabbiness ... throughout", no "ictus" [Cited S2]; Prout ("finest part ... its rich lower register") vs Schlesinger ("hollow and wanting in power") as quoted by Forsyth [Cited S2]; "The lower register, which contains the richest and fullest notes, is ... the most valuable" [Cited S3]; "quiet; smooth and rich. Played loud and short it rasps almost like a synth" [Cited S18].
- Low notes: "the best notes are the lowest ones; but, owing to the slowness of the vibrations, they should not be made to follow each other too rapidly" [Cited S4].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits
- Pianissimo: "phenomenal powers of" [crescendo/diminuendo, printed as hairpin signs that the OCR garbles]; "Its *ppp* is almost a silence" [Cited S2]; for that reason the unaccompanied bassoon solo before the Allegro vivo in Tchaikovsky's *Pathétique* "is usually transferred to the Bass-Clarinet" [Cited S2] (its *pppppp* marking is inside the music example, which the OCR garbles [Estimate: well-known marking]); it "can pass from a forte to a pianissimo as easily as the standard instrument" [Cited S3].
- Low register: richest and most characteristic [Cited S3, S4]; "dark and immovable tone-colour" for doubt and unease (Wagner) [Cited S2].
- Top: "the top fifth is scarcely worth writing, except ... doubled by some other instrument" (1914 compass) [Cited S2]; not destined to replace the high clarinets [Cited S4].
- Character: suits smooth legato and simple scale passages (Tchaikovsky, Sugar Plum Fairy); "solo subjects ... which call for strong rhythmic energy or heroic expression, are quite unsuited" [Cited S2]; "admirable melodic instrument" [Cited S3].
- Agility: technique "practically the same" as the B♭/A clarinet "with some limitations of agility" [Cited S2]; Strauss's *Sinfonia Domestica* uses its chromatic power freely [Cited S2].
- Breath: the wide bore needs more air than the soprano [Estimate: inferred from S2's "large reed, wide bore"; no figure read].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)
- Rimsky: in pairs the bass clarinet is bracketed under player II, in threes under player III (no extra player); in fours it is a separate player IV [Cited S1].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources
1. **Bass clarinet top note.** S7 infobox: sounding D6 (86); S7 text: fingerings (Aber and Lerstad) up to sounding B♭6 (94), but the highest note "commonly encountered in modern solo literature" is written E6 = sounding D5 (74); S5 range table: B♭5 (82); S11: "a²" (81) with professionals higher; Forsyth 1914 compass top about F5 (77) with the top fifth "scarcely worth writing"; libraries sample to 73–76. Recommended playable top 86 (the dedicated article's infobox) and comfortable top 74 (S7's text, cited). The infobox top and the text's "common" top disagree by an octave; a modern handbook (not readable here) should settle the playable top.
2. **Bass clarinet bottom note depends on the instrument** (written note names, sounding MIDI numbers in parentheses). Written E3 (38) on an unextended instrument, E♭3 (37) on "most modern Boehm" instruments, D (36) on Buffet's Tosca BC1185 (whose page title says "descending to D" while its older URL says "e-flat"), C (34) on professional models (Yamaha YCL-622II, Buffet BC1195). Sonara's model has one playable range; recommended 34 with a note, or a later per-instrument extension field.
3. **One transposition field, two bass-clarinet notations.** French treble notation is −14; German bass-clef notation is −2 with treble clef as an upward continuation (S2, S7). Rendering a German-notation part needs a notation flag that the schema lacks.
4. **Clarinet "tremolo" vs the vocabulary.** The existing `tremolo` id means "rapid repetition of one note (bowed or mallet)"; the clarinet tremolo is a two-note alternation up to an octave (S2). Proposed new id `fingered_tremolo`; other groups (flutes, keyboards, harp) should agree on one id.
5. **`multi_tongue` naming.** Proposed for double/triple tonguing (BBCSO "Multi-tongue", OT "Double/Triple Tongue"). The flute cards spell the same technique `multitongue` and the high-brass cards use `double_tonguing`; one spelling should be chosen across groups before any id is added.
6. **B♭ clarinet comfortable top.** Widor (written A6, sounding 91) vs Forsyth (written B♭6, sounding 92; B and C "just playable"). Recommended 91 (the stricter). Modern players exceed written C7 in solo and contemporary writing; no source read states how far, so 94 stays the playable top.
7. **A vs B♭ timbre.** Widor and S5 hear the A as richer/warmer; Forsyth says nobody can tell. Recommended: same samples, A's only functional difference is sounding C♯3 (49), which B♭ sample sets (lowest 50) cannot play natively.
8. **E♭ clarinet top** (written note names, sounding MIDI numbers in parentheses). S6 infobox written A6 (96) vs "many orchestration books" written G6 (94) vs Forsyth's derived G6 (94) vs OT samples to sounding C♯7 (97, after an inferred octave convention). Recommended playable 96, comfortable 94.
9. **Throat register lower limit.** S10 starts the weak register at written F4; S9 at written G4 or A4. The handbooks' weak-note lists (S2, S3) are images and could not be read.
10. **Images in the handbooks.** Forsyth, Widor and Berlioz give compasses, register boundaries, weak notes, impossible shakes and Widor's *piano* top limit only as music images; the OCR texts keep the prose. A scan-reading pass (IMSLP or archive.org page images) would turn several Estimates into Cited values.
11. **Library octave conventions.** Orchestral Tools does not state its octave naming; C3 = 60 is inferred from its B♭ clarinet starting at "D2" and its Flute 1 starting at "C3" (S16; the flute's lowest note is middle C). BBCSO's range table (S18) gives violins from G3 and celli from C2, which fixes its convention at C4 = 60. VSCO file names use C3 = 60 (`A#2` = 58), while SFZ opcodes in Sonatina use `c4` = 60 (inferred from `d3` = the B♭ clarinet's lowest note).
12. **Unreachable primary sources.** VSL Academy pages returned HTTP 502 or an empty script shell; the MIDI Association's GM 1 table is behind membership, so the GM number is cited from Wikipedia only.
13. **Mutes.** Rimsky: muting clarinets is unnecessary; Berlioz wrapped the clarinet in a leather bag in *Lélio*. `con_sordino` is left out of the clarinet lists.
14. **Niente.** No articulation id exists for *dal niente* / *al niente*; it is a dynamic shape (CC11/CC1 to 0). Whether Sonara wants a dynamic-shape id is a vocabulary decision.
15. **VSCO clarinet key.** The VSCO 2 CE clarinet does not state whether it is a B♭ or an A instrument; its mapped range starts at 50, consistent with a B♭.
