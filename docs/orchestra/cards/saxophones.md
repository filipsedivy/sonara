Conversion notes that apply to all four cards:

- Written pitches used throughout: low Bb = Bb3 = 58, low A (baritone) = A3 = 57, traditional top keyed F = F6 = 89,
  high F# = F#6 = 90, high G (some sopranos) = G6 = 91, written C7 = 96. S4 calls the low note "the written B♭ below
  middle C" and the top keyed F "two and a half octaves above the low B♭" [Cited S4]. The octave numbers come from
  S6 ("written range is B♭3 to F♯6") [Cited S6] and S38 ("b0 bis fis3", German/Helmholtz-style naming of Bb3 to F#6)
  [Cited S38].
- MuseScore's pages list aPitchRange, pPitchRange and transposeChromatic (-2, -9, -14, -21) [Cited S15, S16, S17,
  S18]. That the ranges are sounding MIDI numbers and that transposeChromatic uses our sign convention is not stated on
  the pages [Estimate: inferred because the pPitchRange lows 56, 49, 44, 36 equal the concert lows Ab3, Db3, Ab2, C2
  of S5, S6, S7, S8]. With that reading, its professional range (pPitchRange) top is written C7 for all four sizes,
  and its amateur range (aPitchRange) top is written G6 for all four (arithmetic: top + |transposition|) [Cited
  S15-S18 for the numbers; the written values are arithmetic].
- S37 uses the Italian octave numbering in which Do3 = middle C; with that reading its alto, tenor and baritone ranges
  equal the English pages' sounding ranges exactly [Cited S37; the octave convention is an Estimate: inferred from
  the agreement with S6-S8].

### 1. Identity and names (Czech, English, Italian, German, French; how it appears in a score; family)

| Language | Name | Source |
|---|---|---|
| English | soprano saxophone, in B♭ | [Cited S5] |
| Czech | sopránový saxofon (in B); S36's table uses the compound forms sopránsaxofon, altsaxofon, tenorsaxofon, barytonsaxofon, its text the adjectives (sopránový, altový, tenorový) | [Cited S36] |
| Italian | sassofono soprano, in Si♭ | [Cited S37] |
| German | Sopransaxophon, in B | [Cited S38] |
| French | (saxophone) soprano, en si ♭ | [Cited S39] |
| Score | "S. Sax." | [Cited S15] |

- The generic names are Fr. Saxophone, It. "Sassophone" (Forsyth's 1914 spelling; modern Italian is "sassofono"), Ger.
  Saxophon [Cited S1, S37].
- Invented by Adolphe Sax in the early 1840s, patent of 28 June 1846 [Cited S4].
- Family: Forsyth calls the saxophones hybrids that "might be classified either with the Brass or with the Wood-Wind";
  the tube is brass but the sound comes from a single reed on a clarinet-like mouthpiece [Cited S1, S2]. Forsyth
  places them among the brass-wind hybrids, between the saxhorns and the sarrusophones, and Widor treats them in
  chapter IV, section 4, after the percussion and bells and before the harp [Cited S1, S2]. Sonara files it under
  `woodwinds`: the modern description is "a type of single-reed woodwind instrument with a conical body" [Cited S4].
- It is the smallest of the four saxophones in common use; the sopranino and soprillo are rarer, smaller sizes
  [Cited S5]. It is built straight (held "upward and outward") or curved (a curved neck lets it be held downward)
  [Cited S5].

### 2. Sound production

- A single, slightly convex reed is held by a metal ligature on the mouthpiece; the conical brass tube behaves like an
  open pipe and overblows at the octave, unlike the clarinet's twelfth [Cited S1].
- Pitch is set by many tone holes covered by keys; the fingering is the same on every size, so a player passes easily
  from one size to another [Cited S1]. Berlioz traces the fingering to the flute and oboe [Cited S3]; Widor says the
  mechanism and fingering are much the same as the oboe's [Cited S2].
- The left thumb's octave key opens one of two speaker holes [Cited S7]. The "break" is "from written C# to D in the
  middle of the stave" [Cited S31], i.e. written C#5 (73) to D5 (74) (the C# and D inside the treble staff).
- Vibrato is made with the jaw [Cited S9].
- Berlioz: the high saxophone's tone is "much more penetrating than that of clarinets in B♭ and in C", without the
  shrill brilliancy of the E♭ clarinet, "and as much may be said of the soprano" [Cited S3]. Widor (1906) calls the
  soprano "a rather shrill-toned instrument" [Cited S2]. Widor compares it with an 8 ft organ stop = oboe, clarinet
  [Cited S2].

### 3. Range, transposition and clef (sounding and written; comfortable and extreme; register breaks)

Transposition -2: sounds a major second lower than written [Cited S5, S1, S15]. Treble clef only [Cited S4, S2].

| Point | Written | Sounding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest keyed note | Bb3 = 58 | Ab3 = 56 | [Cited S4, S5, S15] |
| Traditional top keyed note | F6 = 89 | Eb6 = 87 | [Cited S4] (sounding by arithmetic) |
| High F# key (standard on nearly all sopranos made today) | F#6 = 90 | E6 = 88 | [Cited S5: "concert A♭3 to E6 (written low B♭ to high F♯)"; "Nearly all sopranos made today are keyed to high F♯ as standard"] |
| High G key (some recent professional sopranos) | G6 = 91 | F6 = 89 | [Cited S5] |
| Altissimo, professional ceiling in notation software | C7 = 96 | Bb6 = 94 | [Cited S15] |
| Rascher's "four-octave range" claim | Bb7 = 106 | Ab7 = 104 | [Estimate: four octaves above written Bb3, from the book title in S35; no note is named] |

- Altissimo = notes above the keyed range, produced by special fingerings and voicing that let an upper overtone
  dominate; S10 gives no upper limit [Cited S10]. S34 defines it as notes above written F#6 [Cited S34].
- Recommended: playable 56-94 (written Bb3-C7) and comfortable 56-87 (written Bb3-F6). See the open questions for the
  choice of ceiling.
- Widor (1906): soprano and baritone "stop at E♭ in the highest register" while alto and tenor reach F; the soprano and
  tenor "cannot descend very easily, so it is better to avoid writing the very low notes for them" [Cited S2].
- Forsyth (1914): the low B♭ key was "regularly fitted to French instruments" but "not in general use" in England
  [Cited S1]. Both are historical limits superseded by S4/S5 [Estimate: modern instruments, per S4, S5].
- S37 gives the soprano as La♭2-Fa5 (= Ab3-F6 sounding), which contradicts its own "a fifth above the alto" (= Ab3-E6);
  not used [Cited S37].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

- One player per part [Estimate: wind practice; no source read shows two sopranos on one part].
- Big-band saxophonists often double on other woodwinds, soprano saxophone included [Cited S29].
- Ravel's *Boléro*: both the sopranino and soprano parts are played on the B♭ soprano, and the soprano player doubles
  tenor [Cited S33].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- Repertoire: Strauss *Sinfonia Domestica*, Ravel *Boléro*, d'Indy *Fervaal* [Cited S5]; Saint-Saëns *La Jeunesse
  d'Hercule*; Holbrooke *Apollo and the Seaman* (soprano in thirds with the E♭ clarinet) [Cited S1].
- Military-band role (1906): reinforce or replace the clarinets [Cited S2].
- Jazz: Sidney Bechet, Steve Lacy, John Coltrane [Cited S5].

### 8. Variants and related instruments (when a player changes instrument)

- Sopranino (E♭; Ravel's F sopranino never existed) [Cited S1, S33]; soprano in C of the orchestral F/C series
  [Cited S1, S2].
- Saxello: VCSL records a "Saxello" (a soprano-family variant) [Cited S23].
- The fingering is identical across sizes, so changes between sizes are easy [Cited S1].

### 9. General MIDI and existing Sonara metadata

- GM Level 1 program 65 "Soprano Sax" (Reed group 65-72; 1-128 numbering, 0-127 internally) [Cited S13, S44]. The
  MIDI Association page opened shows no table [Cited S14].
- data.rs has 16 instruments and no saxophone; gm.rs holds only the GM percussion map [Cited S40]. The vocabulary has

### 2. Sound production

- Single reed, conical brass tube overblowing at the octave, identical fingering to the other sizes [Cited S1].
- Written register break C#-D [Cited S31]; the written C# and nearby notes are "annoying notes" with few keys down,
  and the sound thins as fewer keys are pressed [Cited S31].
- Colour: Berlioz hears the saxophones as "soft and penetrating in the higher part, full and rich in the lower part",
  with a "profoundly expressive" medium and vague analogies to cello, clarinet and cor anglais "invested with a brazen
  tinge" [Cited S3]. Widor: the alto's tone is even, it rises easily and has good bass notes; its organ-stop analogue is
  12 ft = cor anglais [Cited S2]. Altos and sopranos are brighter than the warmer, breathier tenor and baritone
  [Cited S31].
- Jaw vibrato; classical players often model their vibrato on violinists, Mule and Rascher had contrasting styles
  [Cited S9].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

- One player per part; the big-band "sax section" is five different parts: "5 Saxophones (2 altos, 2 tenors,
  1 baritone)", and "Alto 1 is the leader of the section" [Cited S30]. Stan Kenton's 1940s band used two altos, two
  tenors and one baritone; first alto is one of the lead parts [Cited S29]. Early (1920s-30s) bands had three or four
  saxophones [Cited S29].
- Doubling: big-band saxophonists often double flute, piccolo, clarinet, bass clarinet and soprano sax [Cited S29].
  Clarinettists master the saxophone quickly [Cited S3].
- Stravinsky's *Ebony Concerto* uses two altos [Cited S6].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- French composers wrote "little cantilenas for the E♭-Alto"; Bizet's *L'Arlésienne* cantabile is its best-known
  orchestral solo [Cited S1]. Widor lists *Hamlet*, *L'Arlésienne*, *Hérodiade*, *Werther* [Cited S2].
- Twentieth-century solos and parts: Mussorgsky/Ravel *Pictures at an Exhibition* ("Il vecchio castello"),
  Rachmaninoff *Symphonic Dances* (first movement), Berg (*Der Wein*, *Lulu*, Violin Concerto), Gershwin (*Rhapsody in
  Blue*, *An American in Paris*), Britten *Sinfonia da Requiem*, Bernstein *Symphonic Dances from West Side Story*,
  Debussy *Rhapsody*; Strauss *Sinfonia Domestica* uses an alto in F [Cited S6]. Prokofiev's *Lieutenant Kijé*, *Romeo
  and Juliet* and *Alexander Nevsky* use the saxophone [Cited S4] (the tenor in the first two [Cited S7]).
- In four-part harmony saxophones sound like divided violas and cellos with clarinets and bassoons, or like organ
  diapasons; the quartet "produces the illusion of the Organ" [Cited S1, S2].
- Jazz: Don Redman, Jimmy Dorsey, Johnny Hodges, Benny Carter, Charlie Parker [Cited S6]. In big band writing the
  baritone often doubles the lead alto an octave lower [Estimate: search snippet only, not a page read].

### 2. Sound production

- Single reed, conical brass tube, folded for the player's convenience ("the twists and turns in the tubes of the
  heavier instruments") [Cited S1].
- Its mechanism "is not quite so simple as that of the Saxophones of higher pitch" [Cited S2].
- Colour: Berlioz singles out the baritone and bass for swelling and diminishing, giving low effects "bearing some
  resemblance to those of the 'expressive organ'"; bass notes of "calm... pontifical grandeur" [Cited S3]. Widor's organ
  analogue: 24 ft = bass clarinet [Cited S2]. Warmer and breathier than alto and soprano [Cited S31].

### 3. Range, transposition and clef (sounding and written; comfortable and extreme; register breaks)

Transposition -21: "an octave and a major sixth lower than written", one octave below the alto [Cited S8]; Taming the
Saxophone: transposed parts "Up a major 13" [Cited S32]; MuseScore transposeChromatic -21 [Cited S18]. Written in
treble clef "as with all saxophones"; players read bass-clef parts by clef substitution (read as treble, add three
sharps) [Cited S8]; S31 gives the reverse for arrangers: to read a written baritone part at concert pitch, put a bass
clef before it and add three flats [Cited S31]. In a concert-pitch score MuseScore switches the baritone to bass clef
(concertClef "F", transposingClef "G") [Cited S18].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- Repertoire: Strauss *Sinfonia Domestica* (baritone in F), Bartók *The Wooden Prince*, Ives Symphony No. 4, Gershwin

## Open questions and disagreements between sources

1. **Playable ceiling (altissimo).** Sources disagree: the keyed top is written F6, or F#6 with the now-common key
   [Cited S4, S5, S6]; S10 sets no ceiling for altissimo [Cited S10]; Rascher's method claims a "four-octave range"
   [Cited S35] (written Bb7 if counted from the low Bb [Estimate: S35 names no note]); MuseScore's professional range
   stops at written C7 for all four sizes [Cited S15-S18]. The free VCSL tenor reaches written F#7 (sounding E6)
   [Estimate: measured by the fact-check, see the tenor card]. **Recommended: written C7** as the playable top,
   because it is the only numeric ceiling read, it keeps common lead-alto and tenor altissimo inside `playable`, and
   higher notes are specialist reach [Estimate: judgement]. A handbook ceiling (Adler, Blatter) was not readable in
   this session.
2. **Comfortable top: written F6 vs F#6.** S4 calls F the traditional top keyed note [Cited S4]; S31 says big band writing
   goes no higher than F# [Cited S31]; S32 warns that the top three or four semitones are unreliable in a section
   [Cited S32]. **Recommended: F6**, so a written F#6 is reported as `OutsideComfortable` (still playable) [Estimate: design judgement between S4's F and S31's F#].
   The JSON `comfortable` provenance cites the endpoints (the traditional keyed range of S4); calling that range
   "comfortable" is this judgement, and Widor's advice to keep the alto's top 5-6 and the tenor's top 4-5 notes for
   virtuosi [Cited S2] argues for a lower comfortable top (about written C6) if `comfortable` is meant strictly.
3. **Baritone low A.** Playable 36 (low A) vs comfortable 37: S8 says most models have the key; S31 says pros need an
   extra key for it; MuseScore uses 37 (amateur) and 36 (professional) [Cited S8, S31, S18].
4. **Historical compasses.** Widor (1906) gives soprano and baritone a top of E♭ and alto and tenor F [Cited S2]; Forsyth
   (1914) says E♮ and F are extra on alto, tenor and baritone and that the low B♭ key was not in general use in England
   [Cited S1]. Modern sources give all four the same keyed range [Cited S4]. The handbooks' compass diagrams were lost in
   the OCR, so their exact endpoints (octaves) are inferred from the text [Estimate: octaves inferred from the prose of S1 and S2; their diagrams were lost in the OCR].
5. **MuseScore amateur range** tops out at written G6 for all four sizes [Cited S15-S18], which S6 calls the first
   altissimo note [Cited S6]; it was not used for `comfortable`.
6. **Italian Wikipedia soprano** "La♭2-Fa5" (= concert F6 top) contradicts its own "a fifth above the alto" [Cited S37];
   not used. Czech Wikipedia lists the B♭ tenor (military family) and, separately, the C tenor ("C-melody", orchestral
   family) [Cited S36]; this is two instruments, not an inconsistency (corrected by the fact-check).
7. **GM numbers** come from two independent secondary tables, Wikipedia (S13) and PG Music (S44), both numbered 1-128
   and agreeing on 65-68 [Cited S13, S44]; the MIDI Association table is in a members-only download not readable here
   [Cited S14].
8. **Logic Studio Horns:** the Sections / Lead Instruments split of the keyswitches is now read from the table
   [Cited S20] (see the alto card). The page gives MIDI note numbers directly ("C1 (MIDI note number 24)"), so Logic's
   display-octave naming does not affect the mapping. Still open: the CC used by "Dynamics via CC" (not named on S19)
   [Cited S19].
9. **VCSL tenor file names** run G#1-E5 [Cited S45]. Pitch analysis of five files shows the names are one octave below
   C4 = 60 naming (C3 = 60 convention), so the set covers sounding 44-88, including about an octave of altissimo above
   the keyed top 76 [Estimate: measured by the fact-check; an SFZ mapping must add 12 to the file-name note]. The
   generated FreePats SFZ (S24) was not opened to see how it maps them.
10. **Vocabulary gaps.** `tremolo` is defined in articulation.rs as rapid repetition of one note [Cited S40], but Widor
    says "all the shakes and tremolos possible on the Oboe can be performed on the Saxophone" [Cited S2]; reading these
    as two-note fingered tremolos is an interpretation [Estimate: wind tremolos are normally fingered], so a
    fingered-tremolo id may be needed.
    `subtone` and `scoop` are proposed as new ids (not in the preferred list). `shake` is not proposed: Logic maps the
    shake slot to "Trill (Saxes)" and NI marks shake as brass [Cited S20, S21]. Altissimo is a register, modelled by the
    range fields, not an articulation [Estimate: design choice].
11. **Orchestral F/C saxophones** (Strauss's alto and baritone in F; the C tenor) have other transpositions; no ids are
    proposed for them here [Cited S1, S6, S8; Estimate: scope].
12. **VSL Academy** (S41) returned HTTP 502 throughout; its saxophone pages should be read to confirm registers and
    library articulations.
