- Score: abbreviated "Picc." in Rimsky-Korsakov's doubling examples [Cited S1]; written in the treble clef [Cited S2] [Cited S4]. Placed at the top of the woodwind block above the flutes [Estimate: standard score order; no source read states it].
- Family: woodwinds; a reedless member of the flute family [Cited S2]. The part is taken by the 2nd or 3rd flute player, who is used to it [Cited S1] [Cited S2].

### 2. Sound production

- A small flute, less than half the length of the concert flute, sounding an octave higher [Cited S2]; 31-33 cm long, about 13 mm bore, two parts (head joint and body), usually no foot joint [Cited S4]. The missing foot joint removes the flute's two bottom semitones [Cited S2].
- Bore: conical [Cited S13]; VSL: head usually cylindrical and body conical, or the reverse [Cited S4]. Boehm mechanism; split-E and F# Brossa mechanisms listed [Cited S4].
- Materials: grenadilla, composite, or metal (least common) [Cited S4]; plastic, resin, brass, nickel silver, silver, hardwoods [Cited S13]; "usually made of wood" in 1906 [Cited S31].
- Tone generation as on the flute: an air jet ("air-reed") strikes the edge of the embouchure hole and excites the air column [Cited S2]. Fingering and the whole technique are the same as the flute's [Cited S2] [Cited S31].
- Intonation: "not quite in tune", with a tendency to flatness in soft melodic use [Cited S31].

Registers (sounding): low D5-G5 dull and rather hollow, occasionally used for a supernatural atmosphere; middle G#5-C7; upper C#7-C8, piercing when played forte; the middle and upper registers are the ones used most often, except the very highest notes [Cited S4]. ### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

| Register (sounding) | Dynamics and colour | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| D5-C#6 (lowest octave) | Weak, "scarcely heard"; better replaced by the flute's second octave unless the feeble tone is wanted | [Cited S3] |
| same | "thin, feeble, disembodied voice"; Forsyth recalls no solo use | [Cited S2] |
| low and middle | tone "so much weaker" than the flute's in the same register, "of little service" there | [Cited S1] |
| second octave | suitable in joyful pieces "in all their gradations" | [Cited S3] |
| top | "whistling, piercing", extraordinarily powerful, does not lend itself to moderate shades | [Cited S1] |
| top | held notes ineffective; even ppp the upper notes lack tenderness | [Cited S2] |
| F#7-G7 | piano very hard; higher notes practically impossible quietly; B7, C8 ff only | [Cited S4] |
| overall | less volume and less brilliant than the flute, but more concentrated and penetrating | [Cited S4] |

- Soft playing is possible lower down: "mere prejudice to think that it should only be played loudly" [Cited S3]; Brahms's *Gesang der Parzen* uses it piano [Cited S2].
- No "scope of greatest expression": Rimsky-Korsakov classes it with the contrabassoon as highly coloured but non-expressive [Cited S1].
- Agility: anything effective on the flute (scales, arpeggios, trills, figures, turns) can be played, but top notes speak with more difficulty than the corresponding flute notes [Cited S2]. Trills possible up to A7 [Cited S4]; Widor: the flute's two highest shakes are impracticable on the piccolo [Cited S31]. Tremolo easy except in the very highest and lowest registers [Cited S4].
- Pitch bending only about a quarter tone because of the narrow embouchure [Cited S4].
- No mute exists for the flutes; Rimsky-Korsakov notes that one "would render great service to the piccolo" [Cited S1].

How the libraries switch and control it:
- Spitfire: keyswitches (keys above the playable range) or UACC, which uses CC32 by default [Cited S10]. Other Spitfire patch types: "Economic Longs/Shorts", "Light resources", "Time machine" [Cited S9].
- VSL Synchron Woodwinds: piccolo, flutes 1-2 and alto flute share mostly the same articulation layout; articulation keyswitches "C1-F1", type switches from "C2"; velocity crossfade on CC1, Expression CC11, vibrato crossfade on Dim.Ctrl (CC20); lyrical legato and auto-speed switch by playing speed [Cited S8]. The page states its naming: keyswitches "are mapped starting from C1 (for Middle C = C4)" [Cited S8], so with C4 = 60 the articulation keyswitches C1-F1 are MIDI 24-29 and the type switches start at C2 = MIDI 36 (bassoons from C6 = 84) [Estimate: arithmetic from the stated convention]. The page is inconsistent about the vibrato crossfade controller: Dim.Ctrl/C is CC20 in most sections but CC30 in one portato sentence [Cited S8].
- Sonatina `Piccolo Solo KS.sfz`: keyswitches c2 Sustain (non-looped), c#2 Sustain (looped), d2 Sustain (looped, decay), d#2 Legato, e2 Marcato (non-looped), f2 Marcato (looped), f#2 Marcato (looped, decay), g2 Staccato [Cited S24]; with the SFZ default C4 = 60 these are MIDI 36-43 [Cited S26].
- Legato in Sonatina is note overlap: the Legato patch has a `trigger=first` group and a `trigger=legato` group with a later sample `offset` and a 0.2 s attack (shown in the flute file of the same set) [Cited S24].
- **Pitch mapping differs between libraries.** VSCO `PiccoloSus.sfz` is keyed 67-91 and `PiccoloStac.sfz` 70-94 [Cited S23], below any sounding piccolo note; its sample at key 67 measures about 787 Hz (G5, MIDI 79) and its sample at key 72 about 1050 Hz (C6, MIDI 84) [Cited S30]. So the VSCO piccolo is keyed at **written** pitch and sounds an octave above the key. Sonatina's `piccolo-c4.wav` is mapped at c5 (72) and measures about 525 Hz (C5, MIDI 72), so Sonatina is keyed at **sounding** pitch, 72-103 [Cited S24] [Cited S30].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

- Classical tradition: two flutes, the 2nd taking the piccolo when necessary [Cited S2]. Rimsky-Korsakov's woodwind formations: in pairs "(II--Piccolo)", in threes "(III--Piccolo)", in fours "1 Piccolo (IV)" [Cited S1].
- Modern orchestra: "one or two Piccolos are used at discretion", mostly for incidental passages; with three flutes the 3rd (or 2nd and 3rd) player takes them [Cited S2]. Two piccolos: Gluck (*Iphigénie en Tauride*), Weber (*Freischütz*), Berlioz (*Faust*), Strauss (*Also sprach Zarathustra*) [Cited S2]; a composer may write for two piccolos without adding players [Cited S1].
- Always a solo instrument in practice; no divisi [Estimate: one player per part in all formations read]. Libraries record it solo (Spitfire "Piccolo Flute", Sonatina "Solo Piccolo") [Cited S9] [Cited S24].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- Extends the flute's range upward [Cited S1]; can continue the flute's scale so that it sounds like "one flute of extraordinary compass" [Cited S3].
- Octave doublings: doubled an octave lower by flute, oboe or clarinet [Cited S1]; an octave above the flute, an octave apart from oboe or clarinet, octave doubling of tutti melody lines [Cited S4]; doubles the violins or the flutes [Cited S13]. It sometimes doubles a woodwind phrase at the 16th with the middle octave left out [Cited S2].
- Extreme top notes of the flutes are often better doubled by the piccolo **in unison** than an octave higher [Cited S2].
- Effects: storms (Beethoven's 6th), lightning (Verdi, *Rigoletto*); with bass or snare drum for a military flavour; with cymbals for short, piercing accents [Cited S4] [Cited S3].
- Mechanical, bright parts suit it; vocal melodies do not [Cited S2] [Cited S31].

### 8. Variants and related instruments (when a player changes instrument)

- Piccolo in Db, sounding a minor ninth above written [Cited S4]; early 20th-century Db piccolos, used by Sousa [Cited S13]; Czech Wikipedia: tuned in C, exceptionally in Db [Cited S28]. Berlioz's military "minor ninth piccolo flute" (its written C sounds Db; "said to be in Eb", Berlioz classes it in Db) and "tenth piccolo flute" (written C sounds Eb; "said to be in F", Berlioz calls it in Eb) [Cited S3]. Eb military piccolo and the fife (military bands) [Cited S2].
- Player change: flute and piccolo can be swapped in a single bar of moderate time; the embouchure change is slight, the pitch of a cold instrument matters more [Cited S2].
- Related ids in this file: `flute` (the player's main instrument).

- Score: "Fl."; "Fl. à 2" for two flutes in unison [Cited S1]. Non-transposing; sometimes loosely called "Flute in D" after its natural D-major scale, a name that is wrong for the orchestral instrument [Cited S2] [Cited S3].
- Family: woodwinds; reedless, sound from an "air-reed" [Cited S2]. Rimsky-Korsakov groups flutes with clarinets as "chest-voice", bright instruments, opposite the nasal oboes and bassoons [Cited S1].

### 2. Sound production

- The player blows an air jet against the sharp edge of the embouchure hole; pitch depends on the length of the air column up to the first open hole [Cited S2]. Nominally a stopped pipe that behaves as an open pipe [Cited S2].
- Three joints: head (parabolic, plugged), body, foot joint; the foot joint adds the two bottom semitones (C#, C) [Cited S2]. 67-68 cm long, mostly cylindrical, about 19 mm bore, Boehm mechanism, open-key (French) or closed-key models [Cited S5]. Boehm's system was patented in 1847 [Cited S11]; Boehm's holes are cut where acoustics require, with keys and rings to reach them [Cited S3].
- Materials: silver, nickel silver, gold, platinum; less often grenadilla, cocus or wood-metal [Cited S5]; brass on cheaper flutes, sterling silver on expensive ones, African blackwood among woods [Cited S11]. Forsyth: wood has the most "flutey" tone, metal speaks most easily [Cited S2]; by 1906 most virtuosi used metal [Cited S31].
- Registers by overblowing: the second octave is overblown from the first; the third uses overblowing plus vent holes [Cited S2]. VSL: overblowing to the 2nd natural gives Eb5-D6, to the 3rd natural D6-A6 [Cited S5].
- The low register has few overtones; listeners often mistake its octave [Cited S2] [Cited S5].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

- Low register: effective but easily covered; double basses in particular blur it; even a string tenuto must be delicate [Cited S2]; double-bass overtones can mask it, several flutes in unison may strengthen it [Cited S5]. The bottom two or three semitones tend to sound out of tune when exposed [Cited S2]. In combination with other winds the flute "only begins to tell from G or A upwards" (written note is an image) [Cited S31].
- Low flute resembles the trumpet played piano [Cited S1] [Cited S2].
- Top: the 36th and 37th degrees (B6, C7) are produced with some difficulty and are harsh; the 37th cannot be played piano at all [Cited S31]; B and C should not be written pianissimo, while Bb comes out easily and can be held as piano as desired [Cited S3]; top B and C "should not be written piano" [Cited S2]. The MIS pp scales stop at B6 (vibrato) and Bb6 (non-vibrato) [Cited S20]. D7 needs a forced attack [Cited S5].
- Agility: "the most agile of all the wind instruments" [Cited S3]; scales, arpeggios, skips, turns, repeated notes are "child's play" to a good player [Cited S2]; leaps between octaves are easier than on the clarinet; legato arpeggios sound well [Cited S1]. Widor: single tonguing reaches about crotchet = 112 in the low register; double tonguing "easily" reaches 144 "at the expense ... of intensity and clearness", and 160 in one quoted example whose note values are an image; in the medium register double tonguing produces a true tremolo like strings [Cited S31].
- Legato is possible across the whole compass, limited by the lungs [Cited S2]; Widor names two legato intervals that are dangerous or impossible piano (shown as an image) [Cited S31].
- Breath: the flute's mouthpiece demands more breath than clarinet or oboe; avoid long sustained tones in slow tempo [Cited S31]; in its lowest notes the player must breathe after every few notes [Cited S2]; all woodwinds need rests to breathe [Cited S1].
- Trills: minor and major trills from D4 to B6 are easy, lower trills (little finger) difficult [Cited S5]; nearly all shakes are possible, a short list of extreme ones is impossible or very difficult (the list is an image) [Cited S2] [Cited S3]; Widor: all shakes good "from the lowest D to E in alt" [Cited S31].
- Tremolo (fingered, two notes): easy except at the extremes; in the upper register tremolos of a fourth reach A5/D6 and tremolos of a major third reach Eb6/G6 [Cited S5]; Forsyth limits the interval by register (augmented fourth low, major third higher; boundary notes are images) [Cited S2].
- Pitch bending: turning the flute in lowers by about a quarter to three quarters of a tone, turning out raises by about a quarter tone [Cited S5]. Glissando is not a natural flute technique, smoother on open-key flutes [Cited S5].
- Keys: on the old flute few sharps or flats were easiest; on the Boehm flute Db is almost as easy as D [Cited S3]; Widor recommends Db and Ab for sweet melodies [Cited S31].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

- Mozart found one flute enough for his symphonies; the classical tradition required two, the 2nd taking piccolo; "the Symphonic rule to-day is three flutes", the 3rd alternating with piccolo [Cited S2]. Berlioz: ordinary orchestras write two parts, three flutes holding soft chords "would often have an excellent effect" [Cited S3]. Rimsky-Korsakov: 2, 3 or 3 flutes (+ piccolo) in pair, three and four formations [Cited S1].
- The 1st flute never changes instrument; doublings go to the 2nd and 3rd players [Cited S1].
- Each player reads a part; "a 2" writing puts two flutes on one line, for example "Fl. à 2 in the low register" in *Shéhérazade* [Cited S1]. Two flutes in unison or an octave apart sound homogeneous; several in unison strengthen the low register [Cited S5].
- Libraries record both: Spitfire "Flute Solo" and "Flutes a3"; Sonatina "Solo Flute 1", "Solo Flute 2" and "Flutes" [Cited S9] [Cited S24].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- Top voice of the woodwinds; fills the upper harmonies and executes rapid figures [Cited S3]. Often used for melody with violins in its upper register [Cited S5].
- Doubling a soprano or a wind of the same pitch, its natural place is an octave above; it reinforces the first harmonic of oboe or clarinet, and removing it leaves the wind "dull and gloomy" [Cited S31].
- Unison colours: flute + oboe fuller than flute, sweeter than oboe (flute dominates low, oboe high); flute + clarinet fuller than flute, duller than clarinet (flute dominates low); flute + oboe + clarinet very full [Cited S1]. Flute with oboe or clarinet warm in unison or octaves; flute with bassoon one or two octaves apart for an exotic effect [Cited S5].
- String harmonics and flute or piccolo link the strings and woodwinds in the top register [Cited S1].
- Composers often keep flutes too high so that they dominate instead of blending [Cited S3].

### 8. Variants and related instruments (when a player changes instrument)

- Foot joints: C foot (common on student flutes) or B foot (down to B3); the optional "gizmo" key on a B foot helps C7 [Cited S11]. Open-hole ("French") and closed-hole models [Cited S11] [Cited S5].
- Historical and military flutes: flûte d'amour in A, a minor third below [Cited S3]; tierce flute in Eb (Berlioz) [Cited S3]; flutes in Db, Eb, Ab in military bands [Cited S2]; flutes in G, A and Bb below normal pitch, the G one "excellent" (Widor) [Cited S31]; flute in F in Mozart's time [Cited S31].
- Family: piccolo (octave up), alto flute in G (fourth down), bass flute in C (octave down); contra-alto, contrabass, subcontrabass, double contrabass and hyperbass flutes are rarer [Cited S12] [Cited S28].
- Player changes: the 2nd or 3rd flute doubles piccolo or alto flute [Cited S1] [Cited S2]; flute and piccolo can be swapped in a single bar, but a cold instrument plays flat [Cited S2].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

- Colour: "indescribably rich, soft, and velvety"; less brilliant and incisive than the flute but smoother, mellower, more placid, at times more expressive, like the oboe d'amore to the oboe or the viola to the violin [Cited S2]. Rimsky-Korsakov on his "bass flute" (see section 1): the same features as the flute but colder, crystalline in the middle and high regions [Cited S1].
- By register (Forsyth): bottom sixth "remarkably strong", almost like a unison of horn and bassoon; above it, like the flute's middle register but rounder and more tender; the top sixth tends to sharpness and is of poorer quality than the same notes on the flute [Cited S2].
- Balance: Forsyth's example (Holst, *Phantastes*) is accompanied by only twelve muted strings [Cited S2]. Wikipedia: a "rich, mellow tone in the lower portion of its range" and, because the larger bore takes more air volume at a slower airspeed, "a greater dynamic presence in the bottom octave and a half" [Cited S14]; this agrees with Forsyth's "remarkably strong" bottom sixth. The MIS pp scale stops at G5 while mf and ff go to G6 [Cited S21]. [Estimate: strong relative to the flute's own low register, but still light in absolute terms, so the accompaniment stays thin.]
- Agility in the middle and top registers is "not much less" than the flute's [Cited S2]. Breath: the larger bore needs more air volume at a slower airspeed [Cited S14] [Estimate: so phrases are shorter than on the flute; no source read quantifies it].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources

1. **Flute top note: C7 (96) or D7 (98).** Berlioz ("three complete octaves"), Widor (37 degrees) and Forsyth (top B and C hard) give C7 as the classical top [Cited S3] [Cited S31] [Cited S2]; VSL calls B3-D7 the standard range and Wikipedia says modern literature gives D7 [Cited S5] [Cited S11], although the same Wikipedia page's infobox still gives "C4 to C7, although higher notes are possible" [Cited S11]; MIS recorded to Db7 [Cited S20]. Recommendation: playable 98, comfortable top 94. If Sonara wants "playable" to mean "safe for any professional section", 96 is the conservative alternative.
2. **Flute lowest note: B3 or C4.** B3 needs a B foot; Berlioz and Forsyth treat the lowest notes as optional or rare in their time [Cited S3] [Cited S2]; VSL and Wikipedia include B3 [Cited S5] [Cited S11]. Recommendation: playable 59, comfortable 60 (as proposed).
3. **Piccolo top C8.** Berlioz: never write it; Forsyth: fff only; Widor: possible ff and easier than B7; VSL: ff only [Cited S3] [Cited S2] [Cited S31] [Cited S4]. Recommendation: keep it playable (108), comfortable top Bb7 (106).
4. **Piccolo low C5 (72).** Some piccolos have a low C key (Verdi, Mahler) and Sonatina's samples start at C5 [Cited S4] [Cited S24]. Recommendation: keep playable 74; note C5 as an instrument option.
5. **Piccolo comfortable low bound.** 80 (VSL's middle register) is proposed; Berlioz's "lower octave" would put it at 86, and a range-only reading would allow 74 [Cited S4] [Cited S3]. The low register is a balance problem rather than a playing difficulty; Sonara may prefer to express it as a dynamics warning.
6. **Alto flute altissimo.** Wikipedia gives an altissimo to Db7 (97) [Cited S14]; playable is set to G6 (91) as the stated range. Forsyth's comfortable limit note is an image, so the comfortable top A5 (81) is an estimate [Cited S2].
7. **Alto flute low register: strong or weak?** Forsyth calls its bottom sixth "remarkably strong" yet his example uses only muted strings [Cited S2]; Wikipedia gives it "a greater dynamic presence in the bottom octave and a half" [Cited S14]; the MIS pp scale stops at G5 [Cited S21]. Recommendation: treat the low register as the alto flute's strongest and most characteristic, but still keep the accompaniment light (estimate).
8. **Bass flute low end B2 (47) or C3 (48).** Wikipedia: C3, or B2 with a B foot, and many models use a C foot [Cited S15]. Recommendation: playable 47 (consistent with the flute's B-foot convention), comfortable 48.
9. **Flute harmonics.** VSL: B5-A6 harmonics sound almost like normal piano notes [Cited S5]; Forsyth: thinner and "hopelessly flat" on the modern flute [Cited S2]; Widor: only the harmonics on the first 14 degrees are used [Cited S31]. Recommendation: follow VSL (modern practice) and add `harmonics` to the flute.
10. **Double tonguing.** Rimsky-Korsakov says double tonguing "is only possible on the flute" [Cited S1]; Forsyth describes T-K and T-K-T on trumpets and horns as well [Cited S2]. Rimsky's statement is outdated; do not make `multitongue` flute-only.
11. **Vocabulary gaps.** The existing `tremolo` means single-note repetition; the woodwind tremolo of Forsyth and VSL is a fingered two-note alternation, so `fingered_tremolo` is proposed (or the `tremolo` definition must be widened) [Cited S29] [Cited S2] [Cited S5]. Widor calls double-tongued repetition in the flute's medium register "a true tremolo" [Cited S31]; whether the flute should also list `tremolo` (rendered by a repetition or multitongue patch) is left open. New ids also proposed: `multitongue`, `whistle_tone`, `jet_whistle`, `tongue_ram`. The clarinet cards spell the same technique `multi_tongue` and the high-brass cards use `double_tonguing`; one spelling must be chosen across all groups before any id is added. The shared `WIND` list in data.rs cannot express `harmonics` for the flute only.
12. **Written-pitch versus sounding-pitch patches.** The VSCO piccolo is keyed at written pitch, the Sonatina piccolo at sounding pitch [Cited S23] [Cited S24] [Cited S30]. The instrument catalog needs a per-patch "keyed at written or sounding pitch" flag; the `transposition` field makes the shift computable. The VSCO piccolo sample names (`piccolo_G4`, `piccolo_C5`) equal the measured sounding pitch when read with middle C = C3, the convention its flute samples use, so the octave offset is probably a naming slip in the mapping rather than deliberate written-pitch keying [Estimate: inference from S23 and S30]; the practical effect (key k sounds k + 12) is the same.
13. **Keyswitch octave names.** SFZ note names use C4 = 60 when `octave_offset` is left at its default 0, so `sw_lokey=c2` is MIDI 36 [Cited S26]; VSCO's `sw_label` text ("C2 Sustain Non-Vibrato") only repeats the opcode's note name, while its flute sample names use "C3" for middle C [Cited S23] [Cited S30]; VSL's Synchron page states "Middle C = C4", so its C1-F1 keyswitches are MIDI 24-29 [Cited S8]. Do not convert library keyswitch names without checking the library's convention.
14. **Sampler support.** Sonatina's flute Legato patch uses `trigger=first`/`trigger=legato`, `amplfo_*` and `fil_*` (the looped Sustain files were not opened); docs/sample-libraries.md lists filters and CC layers as unsupported and does not mention LFOs or legato triggers [Cited S24] [Cited S29]. Whether the Sonara sampler honours `trigger=legato` needs a code check.
15. **GM for alto and bass flute.** GM has no program for either [Cited S16]; this file uses null and names 74 Flute as a substitute (estimate). The live midi.org page no longer shows the GM 1 table, so program numbers rest on Wikipedia and the Finale manual [Cited S18] [Cited S16] [Cited S17].
