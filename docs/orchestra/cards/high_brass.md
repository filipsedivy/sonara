Conventions used in all cards: MuseScore pitch ranges (S34-S40) are read as sounding MIDI numbers [Estimate: the pages do not say; the lowest values, 52 for every B♭ instrument and 54 for the C trumpet, equal written F#3 at each transposition]. Samplemodeling (S23) and Logic (S20) name middle C "C3"; every number below is converted to C4 = 60, and the original name is quoted. VSL range lines (S3, S4) are sounding pitches [Cited S5: VSL gives the B♭ clarinet as "D3 - B♭6" while its written compass is "E3 - C7"]. SFZ note names in Sonatina (S25) are read with c4 = 60 [Estimate: the SFZ convention; the solo trumpet's lowest mapped key "e3" = 52 equals the B♭ trumpet's lowest sounding note].

**Range rule used for all five cards** (see Open questions): `sounding_playable` is the widest range that a source read states as playable by a professional player; `sounding_comfortable` is the standard compass (the range handbooks and notation software give for ordinary writing). Both numbers, and every disagreeing value, are recorded in section 3 of each card.

### 2. Sound production
- Lip-reed aerophone: the player's lips stretch across the mouthpiece and vibrate like reeds; lip tension ("embouchure") and breath pressure select the notes of the harmonic series [Cited S1 p. 90].
- Bore "mainly narrow and cylindrical"; the bell joint, "something under a quarter of its entire length", is conical; three valves with tuning slides; "the mouthpiece is a hemispherical cup with a rim" [Cited S1 pp. 89-90]. The trumpet's bore is cylindrical up to the bell section, unlike the conical cornet [Cited S13].
- Lowest open note is the 2nd harmonic; the open series runs up to the 8th harmonic; the three valves lower each open note by up to a diminished fifth (6 semitones) and make the compass chromatic [Cited S1 p. 95]. The fundamental is not used [Cited S3, for the C trumpet].
- On the B♭ instrument the 2nd harmonic sounds B♭3 (58) and is written C4 (60) [Estimate: derived from S1 p. 95 (written 2nd harmonic = the C trumpet's middle C) and the -2 transposition of S11].
- "The material of the Trumpet's tube appears to have no effect whatever on its brilliant incisive quality"; the sharper the angle at the bottom of the mouthpiece cup, "the more pungent and steely the tone" [Cited S1 p. 90].
- Rotary-valve ("German") trumpets are common in German and Austrian orchestras [Cited S11]; piston (Périnet) valves elsewhere [Estimate: S11 implies it; not stated as a rule].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits
| Register (written) | Colour and dynamics | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Low F#3-F#4 | "Metallic, dark, substantial, heroic"; forte "sonorous and rounded"; down to C4 "prominent, assertive, dark ... eerie and portentous" | [Cited S3] |
| Low, piano | "the low notes troubled, as though threatening danger" | [Cited S2] |
| Middle G4-F#5 | "brilliant, full, rounded, magnificent"; "its dynamic level equals that of four horns" | [Cited S3] |
| High G5-C6 | "Bright, shrill, penetrating, vivid"; in piano "the high notes are full and silvery" | [Cited S3; S2] |
| All, by dynamic | "Played pp the resonance is sweet; played ff the tone is hard and 'crackling'"; "clear and fairly penetrating in tone, stirring and rousing in forte passages" | [Cited S2] |
- Balance disagreement: VSL says the middle-register trumpet equals four horns [Cited S3]; Rimsky-Korsakov: "To obtain a proper balance in forte passages, two horns are needed to one trumpet" [Cited S2]. Recommended for an orchestration agent: 2 horns per trumpet in forte (the handbook rule; VSL's figure is about the middle register only) [Estimate: judgment].
- Piano is "still clearly audible even in tutti passages"; suited to thematic tasks and solos [Cited S3].
- Agility: "All sort of figures, arpeggios, and scales diatonic and chromatic can be played with astonishing ease and lightness" [Cited S1 p. 98]; runs are produced very easily; large legato and staccato leaps and extemporaneous highest notes are possible [Cited S3].
- Tonguing: single (T), double (T-K) and triple (T-K-T) tonguing "are all possible even in quick tempos" [Cited S1 p. 98]; double tonguing "ta-ka", triple "ta-ta-ka" [Cited S11]; double tonguing only on small-mouthpiece brass, "trumpets and cornets" [Cited S2]; "can be played with great precision at the fastest tempi" [Cited S3].
- Breath: repeated notes belong in the middle register and should be broken up "to give the player time to breathe" [Cited S1 p. 98]. Embouchure endurance (long loud or high passages need rests; lead players tire first) is the main physical limit [Estimate: general brass practice; no source read gives numbers].
- Samplemodeling's dynamic map: a "medium, comfortable" dynamic is CC11 around 60-70, "strong fortissimo" at about 90-95, the highest values "an exceptionally bright, extreme ff sound" for short effects [Cited S23]. Useful as a default CC11 curve for Sonara [Estimate: Sonara's sampler supports CC 11 as expression (a gain), but no CC-driven dynamic layer crossfades [Cited S33: "Not supported: CC-driven layers and crossfades"]].
- B♭ versus C: "the C trumpet sounds brighter, more reserved and more sober", "the B♭ has a softer and more rounded sound" [Cited S3].

### 5. Articulations and techniques, and how MIDI libraries implement them (key switch, CC, separate patch, velocity, note overlap for legato; name the library/manual you read)
New ids not on the preferred list, defined in one line each:
- `scoop`: the note is reached by a short upward slide from below (Logic "Scoop") [Cited S20 for the articulation name].
- `double_tonguing` / `triple_tonguing`: fast repeated or detached notes articulated T-K / T-K-T; a notation-level instruction that libraries realize as repetition patches [Cited S1 p. 98, S7 "Performance repetitions"].
- `mute_cup`, `mute_harmon`, `mute_plunger`, `mute_bucket`: played with that mute; `con_sordino` alone means the straight mute, the classical default [Cited S15].
- `wah_wah`: opening and closing a plunger or a hand over a harmon mute while the note sounds [Cited S15 for the hand over the harmon ("wa-wa"); S23 for the plunger ("wah-wah")].

Mutes in detail:
- The trumpet mute (1914) is "a sort of pear-shaped stopper so constructed as not to alter the pitch"; blown normally it reduces the tone "to a faint echo", overblown it gives "the weird pungent tone-quality"; mark "Con sordino p" or "Con sordino f" (cuivré) [Cited S1 p. 100]. "The Trumpet suffers the mute much more gladly than the other brass instruments ... it preserves ... an unexpected pliancy and agility" [Cited S1 p. 100].
- Muted: piano "very gentle, glassy and subtle"; mezzoforte "enigmatic, eerie"; fortissimo "shrill, piercing, cutting" [Cited S3]. Muted brass give "an effect of distance"; muted trumpets resemble oboe and English horn in quality [Cited S2].
- Straight mute: metal (aluminium) brighter and more forceful than plastic or fibreglass; acts as a high-pass filter passing about 1800 Hz upward on trumpet [Cited S15]. Also solotone ("megaphone-like"), hat/derby (quieter without much distortion), practice and whispa mutes (whispa: softest musical mute, hard in the upper register) [Cited S15]. VSL holds that mutes serve mainly to change the timbre rather than to reduce the volume [Cited S6].
- Players need time to insert or remove a mute; *senza sordino* or *open* cancels it [Cited S15]. Samplemodeling applies a mute change only on the next detached note [Cited S23]. Recommended rest before a mute change: at least about 2 bars at moderate tempo [Estimate: practical rule; no source read gives a duration].
- Logic Studio Horns' keyswitch table has no mute articulation [Cited S20].
- Other Samplemodeling controls: pitch bend ±3 semitones; CC20 attack pitch modulation; CC24 dynamics-to-pitch; CC26 attack, CC27 release; keyswitches occupy MIDI 36-50 ("C1 – D2") [Cited S23].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings
- "The trumpet is the instrument most frequently doubled: Trumpet + Cl., Trumpet + Ob., Trumpet + Fl., as well as Trumpet + Cl. + Ob. + Fl."; doubling a trumpet in the upper octave needs three or four wind instruments, "but in the top register two flutes will suffice" [Cited S2].
- Fanfare-like and heroic phrases fall to trumpets and horns; a phrase too low for trumpets goes to the horns; trumpets, not woodwinds, double a trombone in the octave above; sustained (tenuto) pedals go to two trumpets in octaves [Cited S2].
- In four-part brass harmony the two upper parts go to the trumpets; with 3 trumpets the fourth part goes to a trombone or two horns in unison [Cited S2].
- Low trumpet notes can be replaced by trombones and horns; top-octave notes cannot [Cited S1 p. 99]. Strauss: three trumpets in harmony at the top of their compass (*Ein Heldenleben*); muted trumpets with muted horns (*Till Eulenspiegel*) [Cited S1 pp. 100-101].
- Big band: sections take melody and accompaniment in turn (e.g. saxes the A sections, trumpets the B section) [Cited S17].

### 2. Sound production
- The B♭ piccolo's tubing is half as long as a standard B♭ trumpet's; separate leadpipes tune it to B♭ (shorter) or A (longer) [Cited S12].

### 2. Sound production
- "A military development of the Post-Horn"; tube "partly cylindrical and partly conical"; bore larger than the trumpet's; "a cup-shaped mouthpiece" [Cited S1 p. 103].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources
1. **Range rule (applies to all five cards).** `playable` = the widest range a source read states for a professional player; `comfortable` = the standard compass. For the B♭ trumpet the sources give the top as 82 (S11 standard, S1), 85 (S34 professional), 87 (S3 "occasionally F6", stated for the C trumpet), 91 (S23 "top professional player"). Recommendation: playable 52-91 so that a lead-trumpet written G6/A6 is not rejected, comfortable 52-82 so that anything above written C6 is warned. Owner decision: whether an orchestral profile should cap playable at 87 instead.
2. **C trumpet versus B♭ trumpet top.** The C card's playable top (written F6 = 89, VSL) is lower in written terms than the B♭ card's (written A6 = 93, Samplemodeling) only because the sources differ; VSL adds "F6 and higher". Consider aligning both to written A6.
3. **Forsyth's writing rule** (written C4-G5, "only for special purposes above high G") is stricter than every modern source; it is recorded but not used as `comfortable`.
4. **Yamaha** says the B♭ trumpet ranges from "Concert F# to D two octaves higher" (S32); its low note contradicts S11 and S1 (sounding E3). Not used.
5. **Piccolo valves and low end.** VSL says three Périnet valves (S4) but gives D4 as the bottom, which needs a fourth valve; S12 and S28 say almost all have four. Low end: VSL 62, de.wikipedia "d (max. des)" (62, 61 at most, if read as sounding; S28 does not say), MuseScore 59, Samplemodeling 56 (below the four-valve limit, rejected). Recommended playable low 59, comfortable low 62.
6. **Piccolo comfortable top.** VSL's "sound develops best" C5-C6 (72-84) would warn on ordinary Baroque high parts; the card uses MuseScore's amateur top 86 instead. Repertoire tops for the Brandenburg Concerto No. 2 and the B minor Mass were not checked against a score [Estimate: general repertoire knowledge; no score of either work was opened].
7. **Lip trill versus shake.** VSL: lip trills "cannot be played on the trumpet" (S3); S11 describes rapid alternation between adjacent partials; S23 models a "three-semitones shake". Recommendation: keep `trill` for valve trills and add `shake` for the jazz lip shake.
8. **Balance.** VSL: one middle-register trumpet equals four horns (S3); Rimsky-Korsakov: two horns balance one trumpet in forte (S2). Recommendation: use the handbook's 2:1 for forte tutti.
9. **Mutes as articulations.** This file proposes `mute_cup`, `mute_harmon`, `mute_plunger`, `mute_bucket` and `wah_wah`, with `con_sordino` meaning straight. A cleaner model would be a separate `mute` field (none, straight, cup, harmon stem in/out, plunger, bucket, solotone, practice) combinable with any articulation, as Samplemodeling's CC100 does (S23). Owner decision.
10. **Vocabulary additions without a read source:** `tenuto` (all five), `rip`, and flugelhorn `tenuto`/`marcato` remain Estimates. `scoop`, `double_tonguing`, `triple_tonguing` and the mute ids are newly invented (defined in `trumpet_bb` section 5).
11. **Endurance.** No source read gives numbers for brass endurance or mute-change time; the rest and 2-bar mute-change rules are Estimates.
12. **Names.** Italian "cornetta", "tromba piccola", German "Kornett" and the key suffixes are Estimates; Forsyth's 1914 forms are OCR-garbled. The French table abbreviates cornet as "Cor", which means horn in French scores.
13. **Sampler support.** Checked in code during the fact-check (S43): the parser accepts the `trigger` opcode but maps every value except `release` to an ordinary attack, so Sonatina's `trigger=first` and `trigger=legato` groups would both sound on each note; `ampeg_dynamic` (VSCO) is not supported and is reported in `ignored_opcodes`. The audible effect was not rendered. The key of the VSCO trumpet (B♭ or C) is not stated (S24).
14. **GM.** GM 1 has no cornet, flugelhorn or piccolo trumpet (S18); mapping all three to 57 is an Estimate. GM 2 variation banks were not read.
15. **MuseScore convention.** Reading MuseScore pitch ranges as sounding is an inference from the lowest values (see Conventions).
16. **Forsyth OCR.** His staff examples (exact shake notes, register boundaries) were lost in OCR; only his prose note names are used.
