**Reading conventions used below.**
- Helmholtz names convert with c = C3 = 48, c' = C4 = 60, c'' = C5 = 72, C = C2 = 36, so S32's tenor "E do c²" is E2-C5 = 40-72 and alto "A do es²" is A2-Eb5 = 45-75 [Estimate: standard Helmholtz/Czech conversion; S32 does not state its octave numbering].
- S3-S8 do not state which C is middle C. They are read with C4 = 60 because only that reading is self-consistent: S3 gives the tenor trombone E2-F5 with pedals E1-Bb1 (E2 = 40 is the second partial in 7th position on a 9-foot Bb instrument, S10), S8 calls F1 "the only pedal tone" of a cimbasso in F, and S7 gives the Bb contrabass tuba fundamental as Bb0 [Estimate: internal consistency of S3, S7, S8 with S10 and S12].
- S1's pitch names come without octave numbers; the octaves used here follow S1's own harmonic numbering (tenor ordinary compass from harmonic 2 to harmonic 8 of the Bb1 fundamental, i.e. up to Bb4) and S1's statement that the G bass trombone's bottom note leaves "a gap of two semitones" to the tenor's "first pedal-note (the low Bb)", which fixes that pedal as Bb1 and the G bass bottom as C#2 [Estimate: octave assignment derived from S1's text].
- S27 sample filenames (`tenortbn_sus_A#0` at `lokey=34`) name middle C as C3; the `lokey`/`hikey` numbers are used, not the names [Cited S27]. S28 uses SFZ note names (`e2`, `c6`), read with c4 = 60 [Estimate: SFZ default octave numbering; no `octave_offset` or `note_offset` opcode appears in the S28 files that were read].
- S29 names the tenor trombone range "A#0-C4"; that only fits the instrument if middle C is C3, so S29 names are shifted up one octave here (A#0 = MIDI 34 = Bb1) [Estimate: inferred from the tenor trombone's known first pedal Bb1, S1, S10].
- S31 states "C1 (MIDI note number 24)", which is the C4 = 60 numbering [Cited S31].

### 2. Sound production
- Lip-buzzed cup mouthpiece; tone depends on the proportion of cylindrical tube to conical bell joint and strongly on the player's attack [Cited S1].
- The slide lengthens the air column through seven chromatic positions, each a semitone lower [Cited S1, S10]; tube length about 290 cm in 1st and about 410 cm in 7th position, slide travel about 60 cm [Cited S3].
- Pitch is continuous ("stepless"): quarter- and eighth-tones are possible [Cited S20]; intonation depends on the player's ear alone, as with strings [Cited S1].
- The 5th partial is flat on most trombones and needs a slightly shortened position [Cited S10].
- Bore: small bore = bright, easier high notes (jazz/pop, solo); medium = orchestra 1st chair; large = loud, ensemble blend, large orchestras [Cited S21].
- F attachment (tenorbass trombone): a left-thumb trigger valve that lowers the instrument from Bb to F [Cited S10, S18, S19]; with it engaged the slide has only six positions [Cited S18]. Yamaha describes the tenor trombone with F attachment as "tenorbass" and recommends it for smaller players because far positions can be avoided [Cited S19].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits
| Register | Colour | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Low E2-F3 | dark, weighty; threatening in forte, mysterious and full in piano | [Cited S3] |
| Middle F#3-F4 | metallic and mighty, blaring and heroic in forte; grave and sustaining in piano | [Cited S3] |
| High F#4-F5 | more brilliant, "sweeping intensity"; mellower in piano | [Cited S3] |
| Pedals | metallic, grave, dark, distant (VSL did not sample them) | [Cited S3]; "rather coarse, and wanting in substance" [Cited S1] |
- "Dark and threatening in the deepest register, brilliant and triumphant in the high compass"; piano "full but somewhat heavy", forte "powerful and sonorous" [Cited S2].
- Balance in forte: 1 trumpet = 1 trombone = 1 tuba = 2 horns [Cited S2].
- E2-F#2 are weaker than the same notes on the bass trombone and slower to speak; the slide travel grows with each lower position [Cited S1].
- Agility: single tonguing to MM 120 (sixteenths), double to MM 144, triple "very fast" [Cited S3]. Rapid alternation between 1st and 7th position is "not only awkward but impossible" [Cited S1]. Rimsky-Korsakov: trombones are rarely asked for quick passages [Cited S2].
- Dynamics: sforzando, sforzandissimo, fortepiano; vibrato usable from pp to ff [Cited S3]. 1914 English players often let a forte note "bark out and then dwindle" rather than sustain [Cited S1].
- Breath: long fortissimo low notes tire the player; S1 gives f and p "serviceable limits" for tenor and bass trombone only as a music example (image, not read) [Cited S1 for the existence of the limits; values not available].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)
- Standard: two tenor trombones and one bass trombone, plus one tuba [Cited S1, S10]; Rimsky-Korsakov's middle and large orchestras list "3 Trombones" [Cited S2]. French custom from about 1830: three tenors [Cited S1]. A fourth trombone was often added and kept in 20th-century orchestras [Cited S3].
- Alto, tenor and bass: Beethoven scores this trio in the finale of Symphony No. 5 (1804-1808), his first symphonic use of trombones and a work of his middle period, and in movements 2 and 4 of No. 9 (1822-1824) [Cited S37, S38]. By 1914 the alto was "practically obsolete" [Cited S1].
- Pedal unisons: Hérold wrote a held pedal Bb for three tenor trombones in unison; Elgar's *Cockaigne* has two tenors in unison on pedal Bb with tuba and double bassoon [Cited S1].
- Libraries record solo, a2 sections and ensembles (S25 "Tenor Trombones a2", S29 "Trombone Ensemble") [Cited S25, S29].
- Film sessions: 2-3 tenors plus 1-2 bass trombones, chamber music one trombone in a brass quintet [Estimate: session practice; no source read gives these counts].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings
- Harmonic foundation, melody alone or combined, bass lines with double basses, reinforcement of voices [Cited S3]. Historically each chorale part doubled by a trombone in Bach's church music [Cited S1].

Dependent second valves (Olds 1937 E; Bach 1961 E, later Eb and D) work only with valve 1 [Cited S11].

### 1. Identity and names (Czech, English, Italian, German, French; how it appears in a score; family)
- The word is Italian, probably from *corno basso* / *corno di basso* ("bass horn") [Cited S8, S13]. The same name is used in English and German, where it later also meant slide contrabass trombones in F [Cited S13]. Czech, French: "cimbasso" [Estimate: no Czech or French source opened].

### 2. Sound production
- Modern instrument most often in F, sometimes Eb, low C or Bb [Cited S13]; Wessex says most often F or BBb [Cited S23]; VSL: F and Eb [Cited S8].
- Four to six rotary valves (occasionally pistons) [Cited S13]; Thein: F, five rotary valves, 5th valve = 1¼ tone, bore 14.9 mm, bell 270 mm [Cited S22]; Wessex CB90P F/Eb: five rotary valves plus tuning trigger [Cited S23] (the S23 page gives no bore or bell size). - Bell over the player's left shoulder, facing forward like a trombone's [Cited S13]; "forward-facing bell" [Cited S23].
- The modern cimbasso "emerged in Germany in 1985" from the maker Josef Meinl, a contrabass trombone in F with the valves and fingering of a modern F tuba, descended from Pelitti's trombone Verdi, and was "quickly adopted" by players and makers, including Thein [Cited S13]. - Tone: the design lends its sound "to the bass of the trombone family rather than the tuba" [Cited S13]; "pungent, clear, and focused… bright, and cutting" versus the tuba's "mellow and round" [Cited S23]; slim and flexible [Cited S22].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources
1. **Tenor trombone comfortable top.** D5 = 74 (S10, a "conservative" practical top, kept in data.rs), C5 = 72 (S32, textbook range) and Bb4 = 70 (S1, 1914: "confine the upward compass to the Bb"). Recommendation: keep 74, because two of three sources and modern practice reach above Bb4, but note that S10's value is a practical limit rather than a comfort limit; C5 is the most defensible "ordinary writing" value if a stricter check is wanted.
2. **Tenor or tenorbass?** Many tenor trombones carry an F attachment (S18, S19), which fills C2-Eb2 (S10). `trombone_tenor` currently models the straight tenor (playable from E2). Should the F attachment be a variant id (`trombone_tenor_f`), a flag, or folded into the tenor's range?
3. **Pedal register and gaps.** The tenor's pedals E1-Bb1 (S3) are separated from E2 by a gap on a straight instrument (S10). The data model has one contiguous playable range; an optional "extended" range or a gap list would represent pedals, tuba pedals (S6) and euphonium pedals (S14). The cards are not uniform: `trombone_tenor` excludes pedals (gap, data.rs), while `trombone_bass` (Bb0, S4), `tuba` (A0, S7), `cimbasso` (F1, S8) and, after the fact-check, `euphonium` (B0, S14) include them, because on those instruments the valves make the range continuous. A part checker that wants "no pedals" needs a separate floor: bass trombone B1 (35), the first valve note above the Bb1 pedal (S11), euphonium B1 (35) on a compensating four-valve instrument (S14), cimbasso F#1 (30) (S8).
4. **One `tuba` id for four sizes.** The composite playable range A0-G4 mixes the contrabass tuba's bottom (S7) with the F tuba's top (S6). Alternatives: F tuba only D1-G4 (26-67, S6); Bb contrabass A0-B3 (21-59, S7); Wikipedia's solo extremes Bb0-C5 (22-72, S12). Separate ids (`tuba_f`, `tuba_cc`) would be more exact; the orchestral part itself is size-neutral (S1, S12).
5. **Bass trombone top.** VSL gives F5 (S4); Wikipedia gives only "at least Bb4" and treats Kodály's B4 as notable (S11). F5 is kept as the outer limit; it may be too generous for a bass trombone part check.
6. **Trombone legato.** S1 (1914) says there is "no true legato" across slide positions; S3 says seamless legato is possible. Modern libraries record legato transitions (S25, S28, S29). The difference is probably soft-tongued "legato" versus true slur [Estimate].
7. **Glissando limits.** S3 and S4 say "diminished fifth", S10 says "tritone": the same 6 semitones. A Sonara glissando rendered by pitch bend needs a bend range of at least 6 semitones; the sampler bends ±2 (S34).
8. **Cimbasso keys and range.** S13 says most often F; S23 says F or BBb; S8 says F and Eb. Only S8 gives a range (F cimbasso). Valve counts differ (S8: four and five; S13: four to six in its lead, four to five in its Construction section; S22, S23: five). The designer question is resolved by S13's History section, read in the fact-check: the modern cimbasso emerged in 1985 from Josef Meinl and Thein adopted it (consistent with Thein's "since the mid-80s", S22). The Pelitti date differs: S8 says Verdi had Pelitti build the valved contrabass trombone in 1881, S13 says Pelitti produced the trombone Verdi "by 1887"; the two are compatible (1881 commission, in use by Otello 1887) but neither source states both.
9. **Cimbasso "trombone basso" naming.** Verdi's "trombone basso" (S12, S13) must map to `cimbasso`, not `trombone_bass`, when parsing Italian opera scores.
10. **Euphonium range** rests on one source (S14: full working range B0-D5, easy range E2-G4); Forsyth's compass is an image (S1). The playable floor was changed from Bb1 (34) to S14's stated B0 (23) in the fact-check; if Sonara prefers to exclude pedals for the euphonium, use B1 (35) and tag it as derived from S14. Euphonium tonguing and vibrato facts were not found in a technical source.
11. **Notation variants.** All five instruments have transposition 0 in orchestral writing, but brass and wind bands write some of them transposed: euphonium -14 or -2, Eb tuba -21 or -9, Bb tuba -26 or -2 (S6, S7, S12, S14). The data model has one transposition per instrument; a "notation context" would be needed to read band parts.
12. **New articulation ids** used here (`glissando`, `staccatissimo`, `sforzando`, `fortepiano`, `crescendo_swell`, `rip`, `fall`, `doit`, `cuivre`, `vibrato_on`, `growl`, `half_valve`) are all from the preferred-spelling list; none is in Sonara's 15-id vocabulary yet (S34). Mute types (straight, cup, harmon, plunger, bucket, S10) are a parameter of `con_sordino`, not new ids; Spitfire's "Multitongue" (S26) is a tonguing patch, not an articulation id.
13. **VSL octave convention** is inferred, not stated (see reading conventions); Orchestral Tools' names (S29) are inferred to use middle C = C3 and its bass trombone mapping (to G5) exceeds every instrument source, so its ranges describe key mappings, not the instrument.
14. **Licences.** VSCO 2 CE (CC0) covers tenor trombone and tuba only; Sonatina (CC Sampling Plus 1.0, unresolved for bundling per S34) adds bass trombone; no free source for cimbasso or euphonium was found in the trees read (S27, S28).
