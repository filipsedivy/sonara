Family: brass, a valved lip-reed aerophone with a moderately conical bore, Hornbostel-Sachs 423.232 [Cited S6]. Wagner placed the horns among the woodwinds in his scores [Cited S6]. In the score the horns usually sit above the trumpets [Cited S6]; Wagner tuba staves go below the horns when horn players play them [Cited S7].

### 2. Sound production
- Lip reed into a small funnel-shaped mouthpiece, "a truncated cone"; 1st and 3rd players often use a shallower mouthpiece than 2nd and 4th [Cited S1].
- Tube: valve horn about 7 ft 4 in plus an F crook of about 4 ft 4.5 in, 11 ft 8.5 in in all; bell about 1 ft wide; theoretically conical bore, cylindrical valve loops [Cited S1]. Effective length: F horn 360 cm, B♭ horn 270 cm, high F horn 180 cm [Cited S12].
- The player's right hand sits in the bell; hand position adjusts intonation and tone, and full insertion produces stopped notes (section 5) [Cited S1] [Cited S5].
- Notes are harmonics of the tube selected by lip tension, with three valves (rotary on the modern double horn) filling the gaps chromatically [Cited S1] [Cited S5]. In the top octave the harmonics lie close together, which is why the single F horn is insecure high and the B♭ horn was adopted [Cited S6].

- **Sample-library evidence (not a range source).** VSCO 2 CE maps a sustained horn sample at MIDI 33 (A1, file named `A0` because VSCO names middle C "C3") [Cited S20]; Sonatina's horns are mapped 40-77 [Cited S21]. - **Clefs.** Treble and bass clef, written a fifth above sounding in both (new notation) [Cited S5]. Horn parts are normally written without a key signature [Cited S5]; Forsyth instead advises key signatures [Cited S1].
- **Old vs new bass-clef notation.** In old notation, bass-clef notes are written an octave lower and so sound a perfect fourth *above* the written note (horn in F: sounding minus written = +5) [Cited S6] [Cited S5] [Cited S1]. It was usual before about 1920; new notation (bass clef in the same octave rule as treble) replaced it [Cited S6]; Shostakovich still used old notation [Cited S10]. Test: if the 2nd and 4th parts contain notes too low to play, the part is in old notation [Cited S10]. Forsyth called the custom "foolish" [Cited S1]. Sonara needs a per-part `old_notation` flag on import [Estimate: follows from the two conventions coexisting].
- **Other crooks in older scores** (written C, transposition): B♭ alto -2, A -3, G -5, F -7, E -8, E♭ -9, D -10, C basso -12, B♭ basso -14 [Cited S1]. These are notation keys of the same instrument, not separate instruments [Estimate: modern players read all of them on the F/B♭ double horn].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits
| Aspect | Facts |
|---|---|
| Overall tone | "soft, poetical, and full of beauty" [Cited S2]; full, warm, velvety, mellow, homogeneous [Cited S5] |
| Low register | "dark and brilliant" [Cited S2]; most effective piano, very mellow; forte less powerful and rough when forced [Cited S5]; the 4th horn tends to "drop out of sight" in the bottom fifth of its compass [Cited S1] |
| Middle register | resembles the bassoon, and the two blend well [Cited S2]; full and resounding [Cited S5] |
| High register | "round and full" [Cited S2]; "soft as velvet, bright and very intense, brilliant" [Cited S5] |
| Balance | in forte about half as strong as the other brass, equal in piano; mark horns one degree stronger; two horns balance one trumpet or trombone [Cited S2]; trumpets or trombones at pp roughly match horns at p [Cited S5] |
| Typical effects | fortepiano is a typical horn effect; sforzando and sforzandissimo [Cited S5] |
| Agility | "in spite of valves the horn has but little mobility" [Cited S2]; low register: tone emission "too slow and heavy to allow of anything but single-articulation", double and triple tonguing "in the medium registers" [Cited S1]; double and triple tonguing are easy in the middle and upper registers but problematic in the low register and at the very top [Cited S5]; "the deepest notes (B1-E2) develop only very slowly and ponderously" (35-40) [Cited S5] |
| Embouchure limits | lips set for high harmonics do not pass easily to low ones; a sudden change of tessitura defeats the player, hence high and low pairs [Cited S1] |
| Endurance | an assistant doubles the first part to rest the principal [Cited S6] |
| Vibrato | largely absent from the classical repertoire, common in contemporary music [Cited S5] |
| Double-horn sides | F side "deep, warm", B♭ side "higher, brighter" [Cited S6] |

**Switching and controllers.**
- Spitfire (Kontakt) switches articulations by keyswitches (red keys; several held keys layer articulations), by "Shared KS" across palettes on one MIDI channel, or by UACC on CC32 [Cited S16]. Its defaults are CC1 dynamics, CC11 expression, CC7 volume, CC21 vibrato and CC32 articulation [Cited S17]. Studio Brass has up to 4 round robins [Cited S15].
- Sonatina horn keyswitch patches use `sw_lokey=c6`: C6 sustain, D6 marcato, E6 staccato, F6 legato (ensemble); solo C6-G6 for eight variants (three sustains, legato, three marcatos, staccato) [Cited S21]. ### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)
- Classical orchestras have at least two horns; from Beethoven on four was common [Cited S6]. Rimsky-Korsakov: 4 horns with woodwind in pairs or threes; "6 or 8 Horns" with woodwind in fours [Cited S2]. Weber used 8 in *Preciosa*; Wagner wrote for 16 in *Tannhäuser* Act I, 12 of them on stage [Cited S1].
- **Pairs.** Nos. 1 and 3 play the upper parts and Nos. 2 and 4 the lower; chords are "dove-tailed" (1-3-2-4 interleaved), not 1-2 over 3-4 [Cited S1] [Cited S5] [Cited S6]. 2nd and 4th players are "low-note specialists" [Cited S1].
- **Assistant (bumper).** Doubles the first part for loud passages, takes over before a first-horn solo, or alternates on tiring parts [Cited S6].
- **Unison.** Forsyth's examples include passages where "the four Horns play unison phrases" [Cited S1]; calling it a standard effect is [Estimate: inferred from his examples]. In Tchaikovsky 4 a clarinet doubles each upper horn part and two bassoons the 3rd and 4th [Cited S1]. Libraries sample "Horns a4" as one section patch [Cited S15].
- **Film.** The CineBrass review mentions a solo horn, chords by four horns, "the six horns' quiet sustains", and that the makers "hired 12 horn players, more than enough for any occasion" [Cited S22]; it does not mention a two-horn patch, and which edition holds a 12-horn patch is not stated [Estimate: not in the review text]. Film sections of 6-8 for big scores [Estimate: common session practice; no source read gives a count].
- **Divisi.** Not applicable as for strings: each part has its own player; "a2"/"a4" marks unisons [Estimate: notation practice].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings
- Strauss calls the horn probably the instrument that "blends best with all instrumental groups"; it carries the melody, fills in, or plays the bass [Cited S3].
- Blends with the bassoon in the middle register [Cited S2]; with flutes or clarinets in a woodwind ensemble [Cited S6]; standard member of the wind quintet and brass quintet [Cited S6].
- Repeated-note accompaniment, almost always combined with woodwind [Cited S1]; four-horn harmony alone or with strings or woodwind [Cited S1]; muted chromatic harmony [Cited S1].
- Doublings: a clarinet on each upper part and bassoons on the 3rd and 4th (Tchaikovsky 4) [Cited S1]; a bassoon or bass clarinet can support a low 4th horn, at the cost of a new colour [Cited S1].

### 2. Sound production
- Horn mouthpiece (funnel-shaped), conical bore, played by horn players [Cited S3] [Cited S7] [Cited S25]. Bore midway between euphonium and horn [Cited S7]; Alexander model 108: B♭, 4 valves, bore 12.5 mm, bell 230 mm [Cited S13]; about 290 cm of tubing [Cited S25]; "pitch of the euphonium" [Cited S8].
- Four rotary valves worked by the left hand, unlike all other tubas; bell faces upward [Cited S4] [Cited S8]. Wagner's plan: three valves for compass, the fourth a "compensating" valve for intonation in the bottom octave [Cited S1].
- Pedal tones are much easier than on the horn [Cited S6].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)
- A quartet: two tenors in B♭ and two basses in F [Cited S1] [Cited S3] [Cited S7]; "in the symphony orchestra two of each instrument have always been used" [Cited S4]; purists and many professional orchestras keep two B♭ tenors and two F basses [Cited S8].
- Played by horn players: Wagner wrote eight horn parts so that four horn players could switch [Cited S1]; the last two pairs of the eight horns double [Cited S7]; in Bruckner 7-9 players alternate between horn and Wagner tuba [Cited S7].
- Part numbering: Forsyth calls the tenors A1, A2 and the basses B1, B2; in his example of what British players of 1914 actually copied, the "1.2." staff is in treble clef (the B♭-baritone notation he gives for A1, A2) and the "3.4." staff in bass clef at concert pitch (his notation for B1, B2), so his tenors are 1-2 and basses 3-4 [Cited S1] (p.154 image, read by the fact-checker). A 2024 study says the tenors are the odd-numbered tubas (1 and 3) and the basses the even ones (2 and 4) [Cited S24]. The two conflict; Forsyth describes British part-copying, S24 a numbering convention. See open questions.
- VSL's ensemble patch: "two F- and two B♭ instruments" [Cited S19].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings
- Created to bridge the gap between horn and trombone [Cited S7]; with the contrabass trombone and contrabass tuba they carry the Valhalla motif [Cited S3].
- Repertoire: Wagner's Ring, Bruckner 7 (first heard in the slow movement in memory of Wagner), 8, 9, Strauss (*An Alpine Symphony*), also Bartók, Janáček, Schoenberg, Stravinsky [Cited S7].
- Score placement: below the horns when horn players play them; otherwise below the trombones and above the tuba, which is then called "contrabass tuba" [Cited S7].
- Rimsky-Korsakov notes that the Ring's tenor and bass tubas can weigh heavily on the other groups and that Wagner omitted them from *Parsifal* [Cited S2].

### 8. Variants and related instruments (when a player changes instrument)
- Double Wagner tubas: tenor B♭/F and bass F/low B♭ [Cited S4]; "just like a double horn", heavy, slightly duller; compensating doubles exist [Cited S8] [Cited S24]; several makers combine both sizes into one switchable instrument [Cited S7].
- Mahillon designed a version for trombonists [Cited S7]; by 1914 the instruments had "in most countries" been handed from horn players to military saxhorn players or trombonists, with upward bells and a saxhorn-like build [Cited S1].
- Related: horn, euphonium (same pitch), tuba [Cited S7] [Cited S8]. Sibling id: `wagner_tuba_bass`.
- A double instrument does not need its own id: the part, not the instrument, decides tenor or bass [Estimate].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources
1. **Wagner tuba transposition.** Strauss (S3) and VSL (S4) give tenor -2 / bass -7; modern practice per S9 writes both in F (-7); Wagner also used E♭ (-9) and B♭ basso (-14), and bass clef an octave low (-2) (S1, S9); VSL lists -14 and -19 as "older notation". Recommendation: store -2 and -7 as instrument defaults, and make transposition (and the old bass-clef octave) a per-part property on import and export. Owner decision needed on the default for the tenor (-2 vs -7).
2. **Wagner tuba top note.** VSL: tenor B♭5 (82), bass F5 (77); S6: horn-like F5 for both; S8: notes above an unspecified "concert D" are uncertain; S7: "less adept at the highest notes"; Forsyth (S1 p.152): Wagner's designed compass tops at F5 (77) for the tenor and G4 (67) for the bass. The VSL tenor top equals written C6 in B♭; S6 equals written C6 in F. Recommended: playable from VSL, comfortable capped at 74 (tenor) and 67 (bass), both Estimates; the bass cap follows Forsyth's designed compass.
3. **Wagner tuba bottom.** VSL E♭1 (27) vs S8 E1 (28) for pedal notes; S8 also reports a gap around A1 on three-valve basses and sharp notes between B♭1 and E2 on four-valve tenors. The single-interval range model cannot express gaps.
4. **Forsyth's Wagner tuba staves (p.152): resolved by the fact-checker.** Read at the scan's maximum resolution (1599 x 2479; archive.org's IIIF service offers nothing larger), with the staff lines and note bodies located pixel by pixel: tenor B♭2-F5 (46-77), bass B♭1-G4 (34-67), in actual sounds. Now Cited S1 in both cards. A cleaner scan (IMSLP) would still be a useful second look.
5. **Wagner tuba part numbering.** S24 says tenors are tubas 1 and 3, basses 2 and 4; Forsyth's p.154 example (British part-copying practice of 1914) puts the tenors (treble clef) on "1.2." and the basses (bass clef) on "3.4." (S1, image read). Needs a check of the printed scores (Ring, Bruckner 7) before Sonara numbers imported parts.
6. **Horn comfortable range.** Forsyth's explicit all-four comfortable span is only 48-65 (written G3-C5); the proposed 41-72 combines his pair limits. Owner to decide whether `comfortable` means "any horn part" (41-72) or "safe for all four players" (48-65). Note that both ends of 41-72 are notes Forsyth reaches only "on occasion" (1st/3rd down to written C3) or "at a pinch" (2nd/4th up to written G5/A5), and he says to "approach the extreme notes with discretion" (S1 p.122, image read by the fact-checker).
7. **Horn range statements on Wikipedia** ("C2 to C6" without written/sounding; infobox alt text "C two octaves below middle C to E above middle C") contradict S1/S5; not used.
8. **Valve-trill zone** G♯3-C5 (S5) is not marked written or sounding.
9. **Vocabulary extension.** This group needs new ids `stopped`, `cuivre`, `glissando`, `rip`, `fall`, `staccatissimo`, `sforzando`, `fortepiano`, `crescendo_swell`, `trill_whole`, `trill_half`, `portato`, `vibrato_on`, `multiphonic` (all preferred spellings) and two invented ones: `bells_up` (bell raised, Schalltrichter auf) and `multitongue` (rapid notes by double or triple tonguing; Spitfire "Multitongue", VSL "Fast repetitions").
10. **Sampler support.** Sonatina's horn legato (`trigger=first/legato`) and CC1 dynamics crossfades are not in the Sonara sampler's supported list (S26); only VSCO's horn patches play fully today.
11. **Double horn.** No separate id recommended; revisit only if a library ships distinct F-side and B♭-side patches that users ask for by name.
