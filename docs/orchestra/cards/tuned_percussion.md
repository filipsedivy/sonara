**Octave conventions used in this file.**
- S1 states middle C = C4 [Cited S1].
- **Yamaha's percussion spec pages print key indices, not octave names.** Examples: `C16-C76`, `F45-C88`, `C52-E92`.
  - These are read as 88-key piano numbering (A0 = 1, so MIDI = n + 20) [Estimate: anchored on two cited facts below].
  - Anchor 1: the 5-octave marimba is C2-C7 [Cited S7, S25], and Yamaha prints it `C16-C76` [Cited S15]. So C16 = C2.
  - Anchor 2: the 3-octave vibraphone is F3-F6 [Cited S8, S26], and Yamaha prints `F33-F69` [Cited S17]. That check passes.
  - Anchor 3: VSL maps the Yamaha 2500 glockenspiel C4-E7, one octave below its sound [Cited S13]. It therefore sounds C5-E8 = Yamaha `C52-E92` [Cited S18]. That check passes too.
  - So these pages do **not** use the "middle C = C3" vendor convention, and their numbers are sounding pitches.
- VSL states no middle-C convention on the pages read. Its note names are read as C4 = 60 [Estimate: VSL's C2 to C4 timpani range [Cited S12] agrees with Wikipedia's 33-inch drum at C2 and 20-inch drum at C4 [Cited S23] only under that reading]. S5 uses the German "H" for B natural [Cited S5].
- SFZ note names are interpreted differently by different hosts [Cited S39]. Sonara's parser reads `c4` as 60 [Cited S40: `sfz.rs` line 298].
- In this file, `[Cited Sx, Sy]` means that each listed source was opened and states the fact. Arithmetic on cited facts, such as MIDI conversion or written = sounding − transposition, is exact. An octave placement inferred from letters is tagged Estimate.

### 2. Sound production

- A calf or plastic head is stretched over a copper bowl [Cited S2, S4]. Forsyth's drums used calf, and the bowl has a hole at the bottom [Cited S2].
- Tuning methods:
  - historically by T-handled screws around the rim [Cited S2, S3];
  - today by a foot pedal [Cited S4, S5, S23]. The pedal systems are ratchet clutch, balanced action and friction clutch [Cited S23].
- A gauge on the rim gives a rough visual check of the pitch [Cited S5, S23]. It drifts with transport, temperature and humidity [Cited S23]. Final tuning is by ear [Cited S5].
- Sticks:
  - normally felt over a wooden core [Cited S23];
  - wood, cork, flannel and leather heads also exist [Cited S5, S23];
  - Berlioz: wooden ends are harsh and dry, leather-covered ends less so, sponge ends best for soft and velvety playing [Cited S3].
  - A small head stimulates the higher (inharmonic) partials and sounds brighter and more incisive; a broad head stimulates the deeper partials, and the pitch appears more definite [Cited S5]. Mallet choice is a control and is modelled as a patch or parameter, not an articulation id [Estimate: design proposal].
- Striking spot:
  - about halfway between rim and centre [Cited S2], or "a hand-width from the rim" [Cited S5];
  - striking nearer the centre makes the pitch less definite [Cited S5].
- Decay at mf: about 4-5 s on the large drum and 3-4 s on the small one [Cited S5].

Timpani are non-transposing and written in bass clef [Cited S5]. Until about 1800 they were written as transposing instruments on C and G [Cited S2, S3, S5, S23].

- **How the Yamaha letters were read.** They mix capitals and lower case without octave marks: "b-g" for 24 in cannot be B3-G4. So they are read as pitch classes, and the octaves follow from size order [Estimate: inferred from the minor-sixth spans and the size order; S14 prints no octave marks].
  - Fact-check note: five of the seven entries ("D-B♭", "F-d♭", "A♭-e", "A-f", "c-a♭") read directly as Helmholtz notation (capital = the C2 octave, lower case = the C3 octave) and give exactly the MIDI numbers in the table [Cited S14 for the letters; the Helmholtz reading is an Estimate]. Only "e-c" (top c' without its prime) and "b-g" (lower-case b for B2) need the pitch-class reading. This supports the octave placement.
  - Every model spans exactly a minor sixth (8 semitones), which fits VSL's "about a sixth" per drum [Cited S5].
  - The ends of the scale fit too: a 20-inch drum reaches C4 and a 33-inch drum reaches C2 [Cited S23].
- **Per-drum compass, recorded disagreement.**
  - Wikipedia: "a perfect fifth, or seven semitones" [Cited S23].
  - VSL: "about a sixth" [Cited S5]. Yamaha: a minor sixth on every model [Cited S14].
  - Forsyth and Rimsky-Korsakov, for hand-tuned drums: a fifth (Bb–F and F–C) [Cited S1, S2].
  - VSL also says that on a pedal drum "roughly half an octave can be played as a scale (maximum range: a major sixth)" [Cited S5].
  - Forsyth, for a three-drum set: the composer "rarely needs the wide compass of a perfect fifth" and "need scarcely go outside the limits of a fourth for any one Drum"; the two highest semitones of the small drum "are by no means pleasing to all ears" [Cited S2].
  - Recommendation: plan on a perfect fifth per drum in a careful part, and use the sixth only at the ends of a drum's compass [Estimate: the drum's best tone is in the middle of its compass [Cited S5]].
- **Older sets.**
  - Beethoven's drums: large F2-C3 (41-48), small B♭2-F3 (46-53) [Cited S1]; Forsyth gives the same pitch letters in his prose ("from Bb to F", "from F to C") [Cited S2]. VSL: historical (baroque, classical) timpani were "limited solely to the octave F2 to F3" [Cited S5].
  - Berlioz: the compass of a pair is one octave and nothing below F [Cited S3]. Small drums could give high G, A and B♭ [Cited S3].
  - Forsyth: the middle drum is A–E or G–D [Cited S2]. The low E below the large drum's F "is fairly often called for" [Cited S2]. A large enough drum can give the cello's C [Cited S2].
- Rimsky-Korsakov advised E2-G♯3 (40-56) [Cited S1]. He wrote that a very small drum made for *Mlada* gave "D flat of the fourth octave" [Cited S1]; the list of examples in the same book calls it "Small kettle-drum, in D flat (3rd octave)" [Cited S1]. Its MIDI octave is therefore ambiguous [Estimate: the two passages disagree and Russian octave naming is not defined in S1].
- **Set totals.**
  - The standard four-drum set covers roughly D2-A3; the "orchestral three" (74, 66 and 58 cm) goes down only to F2 [Cited S23].
  - VSL says a group covers "approximately three octaves" [Cited S5]. That conflicts with its own per-drum table, which spans C2-C♯4 [Cited S5]. The table is used here.
- Register colour: the purest tone is in the middle of a drum's compass; high notes sound strained and thin, low notes woolly and weak [Cited S5]. Berlioz: a slack head (a low tuning) is dull and "vapid", a tighter one more sonorous [Cited S3].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

- **Dynamics.**
  - Every shade from a thundering fortissimo to a barely perceptible pianissimo is possible [Cited S1].
  - A roll can carry the most gradual crescendo and diminuendo, as well as sfp and morendo [Cited S1].
  - Forsyth: a crescendo or diminuendo is appreciable to the audience within about eight bars of moderato at the end of a roll [Cited S2]. He warns not to treat timpani as forte-only instruments [Cited S2].
  - At p and mf other instruments are still heard; at f and ff the timpani easily drown them [Cited S5]. Rimsky: their ringing quality prevails in combination [Cited S1].
- **Polyphony.**
  - One player can take a two-note chord; perfect fifths work best, and thirds blur [Cited S2].
  - A simultaneous roll on two notes needs two drummers [Cited S2].
  - Sonara constraint: at most 2 simultaneous notes per timpanist, and a roll on one note at a time [Estimate: from S2].
- **Retuning is the main playability constraint.**
  - Pedal drums: up to quarter note = 150, "a new step of the scale can be played every quarter note" [Cited S5].
  - "The wider the interval the longer it takes to retune" [Cited S5].
  - Hand-tuned drums "needed at least eight bars' rest" [Cited S5].
  - Berlioz: give the player rests in proportion to the change, and choose the new tuning nearest to the old one [Cited S3].
  - Rimsky: with three screw drums, one drum can be retuned "during a pause of some length" [Cited S1].
  - Forsyth: mechanical drums are "more instantaneous than accurate" [Cited S2].
- **Proposed checker rule (all Estimate, derived from the facts above):**
  - Model each drum as one pitch at a time.
  - A drum may change by up to 2 semitones in one beat at ≤150 bpm, while it is not being struck.
  - Larger changes need about 1 beat per 2 semitones plus 1 bar to check the tuning.
  - Without pedals (a "baroque" set), require 8 bars.
  - Any note outside the current drums' compasses (about a fifth each) is a tuning change, not a free choice.
- **Glissando.**
  - A pedal glissando works rising and is weak falling [Cited S5]; Wikipedia agrees that low to high is most effective [Cited S23].
  - A glissando roll works at any dynamic in both directions [Cited S5].
  - A glissando is limited to one drum's compass [Estimate: from S5's per-drum compass].
- Muting: *coperti* with a piece of cloth on the head [Cited S1]; a strip of cloth [Cited S2]; felt, giving reduced resonance and a dry tone [Cited S5]; "con sordino (with mute) or coperti (covered)", with felt or leather mutes [Cited S23]. S5 uses only the word coperto. Mahler opens the third movement of his Symphony No. 1 with 34 bars of muted drums [Cited S2].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

### 2. Sound production

- **Bars:** chromatic wooden bars in two rows laid out like a piano keyboard; the "black-key" row is raised [Cited S6].
  - The bars hang on a string through holes at their nodal points [Cited S6].
  - Materials: rosewood, padauk, cocobolo, or synthetics such as fibreglass-reinforced plastic [Cited S24]. Yamaha builds models in rosewood and in Acoustalon [Cited S16].
- **Resonators:** each bar has its own; they amplify the sound and soften the tone [Cited S6]. Concert models have tube resonators [Cited S24].
- **Mallets** [Cited S6, S24]:
  - hard rubber, polyball or acrylic as standard; wood heads for a lighter tone; yarn or soft cores for softer effects.
  - Harder mallets bring out the upper partials [Cited S6]. Scores usually mark only soft, medium or hard, although eight degrees exist [Cited S6].
- Hugill calls the xylophone "a soprano marimba, with harder wooden bars" [Cited S4].
- Forsyth's English pattern had 27 bars in two rows, like the 27-bar English glockenspiel, and an octave lower [Cited S2]. The older continental pattern had 36 bars in four interlaced rows on straw [Cited S2].

### 2. Sound production

- **Bells:** at least 23 bells in chromatic order [Cited S30, S32], usually cast bronze [Estimate: not stated in the text read]. With fewer than 23 the instrument is a "chime" [Cited S30, S32].
- **Clavier:** batons played with a closed fist, plus 1-2 octaves of pedal keys for the heavy bass bells [Cited S32]. Wires connect the batons to the clappers [Cited S32].
- **Partials:** bell partials are not necessarily harmonic; bellfounders tune five principal tones, notably the minor-third "tierce" [Cited S30, S32].
- **Bell weights:** a bell sounding low C weighs about 5,000 lb [Cited S32]. The Rockefeller bourdon weighs more than 20 tons [Cited S32].
- **Electric action** suits simple clock tunes. No electric system yet allows expression through touch [Cited S32].
- **In Czechia** [Cited S33]:

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

- **Dynamics:** touch-sensitive, with a range similar to the piano's [Cited S30, S32].
- **Sustain:** a struck bell rings until its vibration dies out naturally. Dampers only deaden the sound [Cited S32].
- **Agility:** heavy bass bells limit fast playing in the low register, which is played with the feet [Estimate: from S32's pedal description].
- **Proposed limits** [Estimate: two fists plus two feet]:
  - at most 4 simultaneous notes;
  - no fast repetition on the largest bells.

What a composer writes differently for the carillon:

- **No pedalling and no damping.** A struck bell rings until its vibration dies [Cited S32, S41]: large bells for up to
  about 30 s, small ones sometimes only a few seconds [Cited S41]. A part has no damper-pedal marks; each harmony rings into
  the next, and rapid harmonic changes in the bass blur [Cited S41]. Holding the clapper against the bell mutes it, but players
  advise against it [Cited S41].
- **Voicing around the minor-third partial.** Bells have a prominent minor-third overtone, the tierce [Cited S30, S41].
  A close major triad in the bass bells does not sound major, so major chords are spaced out (C-G-E rather than C-E-G),
  while minor and diminished chords sound natural low down [Cited S41].
- **Dynamics by touch, loudest in the bass.** Touch sets the dynamic [Cited S30, S32]. The large bass bells have more
  dynamic range and the small treble bells reach only a fraction of their volume, so a crescendo works better moving down
  the keyboard, and the melody may go in the bass with the treble accompanying ("think upside down") [Cited S41]. Soft and
  fast at once is hard: the deep key fall (1.6-2.2 in) must be prepared for a pp note [Cited S41].
- **Sustain by tremolo and rolls.** Tremolo answered the carillon's inability to sustain single notes expressively [Cited
  S30]; it is played between the two hands, or with a cluster in each hand [Cited S41]. Rolled chords are idiomatic: an
  upward arrow asks for an open-handed roll, a lightning-bolt sign for a broken roll with alternating fists [Cited S41].
- **One note per fist.** Traditional technique strikes each key with a closed fist, one note per hand, and a four-note
  chord takes two hands and two feet; on lighter claviers one open hand can take two notes up to a fourth apart [Cited S41].
  The keys lie 14 in per octave against the piano's 6.5 in, so fast leaps are hard; fast independent hands stay within
  about two octaves of each other, and fast repeated notes belong to the upper range, not to the heavy bass bells or the
  pedals [Cited S41]. Compared with the concert grand, avoid dense chords in one hand and wide fast leaps [Estimate:
  follows from the facts in this bullet].
- **Range and transposition.** Most pieces are written for, or made playable on, a four-octave C3-C7 carillon without the
  lowest C#3, which suits most concert instruments [Cited S41] (compare the json's 48-96 and the missing-bell note in
  section 3). Many towers transpose, from down a perfect fourth to up an octave [Cited S30, S41], so the same written
  part sounds at a different pitch on each [Estimate: follows from the transposition range].
- **Notation.** Two staves: the top for the manual, read in treble clef, the bottom for the pedals, in bass clef [Cited
  S30, S41]; players prefer 8va or 8vb beyond the third ledger line to a change of clef [Cited S41].

5. **Xylophone notation.**
   - Modern sources give +12 [S6, S24]. Forsyth recommended writing at true pitch [S2], and VSL notes that some scores do [S6].
   - Recommendation: +12, with a per-part "at pitch" override.
6. **Glockenspiel notation.** +24 is standard [S2, S4, S9, S27]. Wagner and some scores use +12 [S2, S9]. The keyboard glockenspiel is +12 [S3, S9].
7. **Glockenspiel range.** The 2½-octave G5-C8 [S4, S9, S18] and the 3-3⅓-octave C5-C8/E8 or F5-F8 [S9, S18] are both current. Hugill calls written G3-C6 "the normal written range" [S4], while VSL says three-octave instruments are "usual in the symphony orchestra" [S9]. The cards propose playable 72-108 and comfortable 79-108 (the compass every listed model covers). Tops above C8 (E8 [S18], F8 [S9]) are left out of `playable`.
8. **Vibraphone range.** Hugill's "F3 to F5" [S4] contradicts F3-F6 in S8, S17 and S26 (S20 says only "3-octave", with no note names), and is treated as a slip. **Pitch bend depth:** "about a half step" [S26] versus "a maximum of a minor or a major second" [S8]; the card plans on a semitone.
9. **Tubular bells octave.**
   - Orchestral sources write the strike note C4-F5 or F3-F5 at pitch [S2, S4, S10, S28].
   - Yamaha numbers its chimes C5-F6 [S19], the case Wikipedia describes as "some manufacturers list the lowest note as C5" [S28].
   - The free libraries map them at three different octaves [S36, S37, S38]. The cards store transposition 0 and 60-77 comfortable.
   - VSL contradicts itself: an 18-tube set C4-F5, but notated pitches that "always range from F3 to F5" [S10]. Hugill also says "usually from F3 to F5" [S4]. Whether a part may assume F3-B3 needs a percussionist's view.
10. **Library octave offsets are needed.**
    - VSCO maps the glockenspiel one octave below its sound and the xylophone 1 octave below; SSO maps the glockenspiel two octaves below [S36, S37]. VSL documents its own one-octave-down mapping [S13].
    - The catalog manifest has no field for a preset's key offset [S40]. Sonara's SFZ parser also does not read `octave_offset`/`note_offset` (they are not in its supported list) [S40].
    - Should `sonara-library.json` gain a per-instrument `key_offset` (and an orchestration id)?
11. **Sonatina percussion in Sonara's sampler.**
    - `xfin/xfout` velocity crossfades (timpani, marimba, crotales), `trigger=release_key` (timpani and marimba rolls), filters (xylophone, glockenspiel) and the CC21 LFO (vibraphone) are all outside the supported opcode list [S37, S40].
    - Layers may double, and roll tails may sound at note-on. This was not run; it should be tested with the real files before SSO percussion is recommended.
12. **Vocabulary questions.**
    - Mallet "rolls" are called rolls by every library [S13, S37], but the vocabulary puts mallet repetition under `tremolo` and keeps `roll` for drums [S40]. The cards use `roll` for timpani and `tremolo` for mallets. Should `roll` be allowed as an alias for mallet instruments?
    - New ids used in this file: `bowed` (proposed here), `pitch_bend` (proposed here), and from the preferred list `let_ring`, `damped`, `dead_stroke`, `glissando`, `crescendo_swell`, `tremolo_measured`, `fortepiano`, `vibrato_on`, `non_vibrato`.
    - Mallet or stick hardness, and the vibraphone motor speed, are proposed as controls, not articulations.
13. **Data.rs `MALLETS` list.** `staccato` is the natural state of the xylophone [S6], but on the glockenspiel it means a damped note [S9, S13]. Should glockenspiel use `damped` instead? `marcato` has no source for any mallet instrument.
14. **Czech names.**
    - "Zvonkohra" is linked to both *Glockenspiel* and *Carillon* [S33, S34]. The cards propose `glockenspiel` as the default in orchestral context, `carillon` for "zvonohra/věžní zvonkohra", and asking when the context is unclear.
    - Czech for tubular bells ("trubicové zvony") is cited only from search snippets [S35]; Czech score practice ("zvony") was not checked.
15. **Carillon modelling.**
    - Family `percussion` vs `keyboards`.
    - The JSON range is a representative C3-C7 instrument (Estimate). Real towers need a per-instance range and transposition.
    - No carillon sample source was searched. The GM1 fallback is 15 "Tubular Bells"; GM2 has variation 2 "Carillon Bells" [S22].
    - Dampers: the JSON `keyboard` object describes the bells as undamped with no damper release, while §4 cites S32 that dampers only deaden the sound [S32]. Whether S32 means that some carillons carry dampers, or that dampers are avoided for that reason, was not re-checked. S41 settles the practice: each key can hold the clapper against the bell to mute it, and players advise against it, so the undamped model stands [S41].
16. **Family inference gaps** [S40]. The keyword list lacks "chimes", "tubular", "crotales", "carillon", "vibes", "glock" and "celeste". Sonatina's `Celeste.sfz` sits in a `Percussion` folder and would be inferred as percussion, while the celesta is a keyboards instrument (family.rs doc comment).
17. **GM coverage.** GM1 has no channel-10 note for any instrument in this group [S21]. The GM2 Orchestra Kit (49) contains timpani, but its note numbers were not read [S22].
18. **Rimsky-Korsakov's Table E** was read by eye from an image. Its endpoints (xylophone low "confused" F3-A3; keyboard glockenspiel G3-C6/D6) are Estimates and should be checked against a clearer scan.
