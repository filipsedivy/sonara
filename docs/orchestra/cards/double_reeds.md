### 2. Sound production

- Small conically bored pipe (cocus, rosewood or ebonite) ending in a bell; a metal staple holds the double reed of two thin cane blades bound together; the reed is the sound producer and the air column reinforces it [Cited S1].
- Conical bore, so it overblows the octave; two speaker keys, the second only venting the highest notes [Cited S1].
- Fundamental scale chromatic from middle C; B3 and Bb3 come from key extensions [Cited S1].
- Single tonguing only because of the reed in the mouth [Cited S1]; double and triple tonguing are hard on the oboe [Cited S5].
- French school: vibrato is integral; Viennese oboe: played without vibrato [Cited S5].

### 4. Dynamics by register, colour by register, agility, breath/bow/physical limits

| Register | Colour | Dynamics | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (Bb3 upwards) | thick, heavy, melancholy; "the lowest develop very powerfully"; pianissimo sounds flute-like | lowest notes strong | [Cited S5] |
| Low fifth | coarse, over-reedy, used as an English-horn effect (Lohengrin Act II: two oboes on low D, crescendo to fortepiano) | | [Cited S1] |
| Low (R-K) | "thick and rough, yet still nasal"; low register "wild" | | [Cited S2] |
| Middle | bright, forceful, reedy; the most expressive oboe sound | | [Cited S5] |
| Upper | less volume, substance and expressiveness the higher it goes | | [Cited S5] |
| Very high | "hard, dry" | | [Cited S2] |

- **Soft low notes.** The lowest notes "develop very powerfully" [Cited S5]; low trills Bb3-D4 respond slowly and sound coarse [Cited S5]; the lowest note cannot be played muted [Cited S2]. A pianissimo entry on Bb3-C4 is risky [Estimate: common oboists' experience; no source read states it for the oboe, but S6 states it for the English horn's low register]. Agent rule: avoid pp attacks below C4 (60) and do not end a soft phrase on Bb3/B3 [Estimate: derived from the facts above].
- **Balance.** In forte, 1 horn = 2 oboes = 2 flutes = 2 clarinets = 2 bassoons; in piano all winds are roughly equal; in piano a whole violin section roughly equals one wind instrument [Cited S2].
- **Agility.** Single tonguing to about MM 140 in sixteenths [Cited S5]; rapid light staccato is a characteristic strength [Cited S1]; octave skips easier than on the clarinet [Cited S2]; arpeggios and rapid legato alternation of two intervals do not sound well on oboes [Cited S2]; agile, "but by no means as agile as the flute" [Cited S5]. The effective compass is small and the best melodies are vocal in type; a violin E-string melody is not an oboe melody [Cited S1].
- **Breath and endurance (the oboe needs rests).** Almost no breath is needed and a sostenuto can be held p or f for a long time, but long sostenuti keep the player in continual tension: only a thin stream of air escapes, so phrases must be broken so the player can "empty and refill his lungs" [Cited S1]. Outside solos the oboe needs on average three or four bars of rest for every bar played, and in a long work should play no more than about a quarter of the bars [Cited S1]. Widor stresses the opposite side: the oboe "excels all its congeners in sustaining-power" and uses far less wind than the flute (Tannhäuser Act III prelude) [Cited S3]. Woodwinds in general need rests from time to time [Cited S2]. The sources agree that long holds are possible but tiring; they differ in emphasis. Agent rule: phrases of up to about 8 bars, then at least a bar of rest; do not use the oboe as a continuous filler [Estimate: derived from S1's ratio].

### 6. Solo vs section (typical number of players, divisi, unison doubling)

- Standard number in symphony and opera orchestras is two; one in small orchestras; "peculiarly a Solo instrument"; since the Ring up to three oboes plus English horn, which leaves the first oboist rested for solos [Cited S1].
- R-K formations: in pairs 2 oboes (II also English horn); in threes 2 oboes + 1 English horn; in fours 3 oboes + 1 English horn [Cited S2]. The first oboe never changes instrument [Cited S2].
- Parsifal 3 oboes + English horn; Gurre-Lieder 4 oboes + 1 English horn [Cited S5].
- Each player has its own part; unison is marked "a 2" [Estimate: notation practice]. SSO ships an "Oboes" section patch beside the solo oboe [Cited S23].
- A single oboe doubling a string tune is one player against perhaps a dozen and is covered, but "a Wood-Wind solidifies a tune on the Strings" [Cited S1].

### 7. Role in the orchestra and typical doublings

- Melodic and solo instrument of cantabile, peaceful character; in quick passages oboes often double flutes, clarinets or strings [Cited S2].
- Long held notes against rhythmic orchestra; humorous or sardonic staccato (Hänsel und Gretel: two oboes in major seconds) [Cited S1].
- Stopped and muted horn and trumpet notes resemble oboe tone and blend with it [Cited S2].
- Model solos: Beethoven, Schubert (C major Symphony Andante), Strauss Don Juan and Don Quixote, Brahms Symphony 1 slow movement, Berlioz Symphonie fantastique [Cited S1].

### 2. Sound production

- Descendant of the alto pommer; wider and longer wooden pipe than the oboe, small globular bell; the metal crook is bent back; the reed is larger and thicker and the air column "much more easily controlled" [Cited S1].
- Fingering practically the same as the oboe, so oboists double on it [Cited S1, S16].

### 2. Sound production

- Modern Heckel type: conical maple pipe over 16 ft, doubled back four times, metal bell pointing downwards, crook like a bassoon's [Cited S1]; Heckel patented the redesign in 1877; joints cannot be taken apart, only the bell is removable [Cited S11]. Played sitting [Cited S11].
- Reed 65-75 mm long vs 53-58 mm for the bassoon [Cited S20].
- Tube about 550 cm with the standard bell (Bb0), about 610 cm with the A bell [Cited S8].
- Older types: French/Austrian brass Mahillon-Schollnast (lacks four bottom semitones) and English Stone-Morton-Haseneier (lacks Bb and B of the lower octave) [Cited S1]; Widor's double bassoon lacks the lowest Bb and B [Cited S3].

### 3. Range, transposition and clef (sounding and written; comfortable and extreme; register breaks)

**Notation an octave above sounding** (transposition -12), bass clef [Cited S1, S8, S20]; tenor clef rarely [Cited S8, S20]; treble even less, "only necessary for the most ambitious solo repertoire" [Cited S20; S8 does not mention treble clef]. Exceptions: Wagner writes it at sounding pitch in Parsifal [Cited S1]; "some of Wagner's and Debussy's scores" sound as written [Cited S8].

## Open questions and disagreements between sources

1. **English horn top.** Wikipedia C6 = 84 (S16) vs VSL A5 = 81 with B5 = 83 extreme (S6), Prout B5 (S4), Forsyth written E6 = sounding A5 (S1). This file keeps 84 as playable and 81 as comfortable. A handbook of the Adler/Blatter type was not readable, so the modern written top (G6?) is unconfirmed [Estimate: G6 is inferred from the oboe-like keywork, not read in a handbook].
2. **Oboe d'amore top.** de.wikipedia sounding E6 = 88 (S18) vs Forsyth written E6 = sounding C#6 = 85 (S1). Kept 88 playable, 85 comfortable. A maker's page (Lorée, Howarth) was not opened.
3. **Bassoon top.** Wikipedia Ab5 in parentheses (S19) and "roughly G5" in the text; VSL F5 in parentheses (S7); Prout, Forsyth and Widor Bb4 as the orchestral or working limit (S4, S1, S3). Proposed comfortable 72 (C5) rather than 70 (Bb4) because the modern source and Forsyth's own footnote say the limit is often exceeded; the owner may prefer 70.
4. **Contrabassoon lowest note in VSL.** VSL's range line says "B0-C4" but its body text says the standard bell's lowest note is Bb0 (S8); probably a lost flat sign. Wikipedia and Heckel give Bb0 (S20, S11).
5. **Prout's contrabassoon limits "D1 and F3"** (S4): the caption adds "Sounding pitches are an octave lower than notated", but does not say whether D1 and F3 name the notated or the sounding notes. As notated they would sound D0-F2, below the instrument's Bb0, so they are most likely sounding values (26-53, written D2-F4), which matches the 19th-century instruments that lacked the four bottom semitones (S1). Not used for any number here.
6. **Contrabassoon staccato.** VSL "surprising agility in staccato" (S8) vs Widor "staccato notes are to be entirely eschewed" (S3, 1904, French instrument). Kept staccato.
7. **Oboe endurance.** Forsyth (S1) asks for 3-4 bars of rest per bar played; Widor (S3) praises the oboe's sustaining power. Both are true (little air, much tension); the agent rule in the oboe card is an Estimate.
8. **Extensions as data.** Low A on bassoon (33) and contrabassoon (21) and low Eb3 on the English horn (51) exist only on some instruments (S7, S11, S16). The current model has one playable range; should Sonara add an optional `extension_low` so that A0 or A1 is "playable with extension" instead of `BelowPlayable`?
9. **Articulation ids.** `fingered_tremolo` is a new id (wind two-note tremolo; the vocabulary's `tremolo` means one-note repetition). `key_click` is used for VSL's "key slap". Both need an owner decision.
10. **GM fallbacks.** GM1 has no oboe d'amore or contrabassoon (S13); the 69/71 fallbacks are Estimates and the GM bassoon patch's low limit is not specified.
11. **Library behaviour in Sonara.** SSO legato (`trigger=legato`), CC1 dynamics and filter opcodes are not played by Sonara's sampler (S25, S26); no SSO or VSCO double-reed patch was rendered or listened to in this research.
12. **Not read:** Adler, Blatter, Piston, Del Mar (copyrighted); the Spitfire manual (S32, text not extractable); the live MIDI Association GM pages (table now behind a login; the archived copy S13 was used); Yamaha's English-horn page (HTTP 403).
13. **Bassoon soft low attacks.** Forsyth praises the low Bb "taken piano" as "velvety" (S1); Widor says in piano passages "the lowest B[?] and D" are "difficult to attack" (S3; the OCR accidental is unreadable). The bassoon card keeps sustained soft low notes but adds an Estimate agent rule against exposed pp attacks on 34-38.
